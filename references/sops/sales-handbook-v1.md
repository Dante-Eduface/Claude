## The Basics

Good sales skills start with good basics. The basics of sales are:

### #1 Common vocabulary and definitions
- **Economic buyer (EB):** person with discretionary use of the funds.
- **Champion:** person with influence AND authority in the account who can get us a meeting with the EB.
- **Coach:** person who can help us answer questions and get meetings inside the account, but WITHOUT influence or authority.
- **POV:** Proof of Value — the pilot we run to prove how Eduface can help the customer.
- **Gates:** the exit criteria for completing each stage of the sales process.
- **Enemy:** the competition, or the competition's champion.

### #2 Listening
We can listen faster than most people speak, so we get impatient. We start thinking of our answers, next questions, or imagine hearing something else. Don't. You may have heard, but do you understand? Do not listen to win, listen to understand.

### #3 Be present
Turn off distractions to be completely present in every conversation. Most people are in a constant state of partial attention. Be fully present.

### #4 Intuition
Not everything is a matter of fact, not everything can be fully analyzed. Sometimes your intuition tells you more about a deal than a customer's answer. Intuition helps us feel an account's status.

### #5 Self-awareness
How does your personality influence the people around you?

### #6 Structure
Work structured and always be on top of your pipeline, priorities, and updates. Follow each step of the handbook and don't skip steps. The handbook is built to create the highest win rates, highest average contract value, and shortest sales cycles.

### #7 Success
Success happens when Eduface is mentioned in a positive light at the dinner table of the people you impacted that day. The husbands and wives of our customers must know how great Eduface is. That is success, so be successful.

## Introduction

The handbook is designed to:
- Clearly identify where an account sits in the sales flow.
- Provide a clear overview of the steps still required.
- Define specific exit criteria (gates) for each sales stage.
- Reveal which stages need additional training or improvement.

## Step 1: Discovery

**Goal:** Qualify accounts into or out of our forecast. Identify pain points, current processes, expectations, key players, and potential business gain.

> *"Discovery is not selling, discovery is investigating."* — John McMahon, CRO

### Discovery questions (by category)

**Problem & Root Cause**
- What's happening today that makes this a problem for your institution?
- Why do you think this issue exists in the first place — process, systems, or resourcing?
- What changed recently that made this more visible or painful?
- Is this isolated to one faculty or program, or consistent across the university?

**Timing & Urgency**
- Why does this need to be addressed now versus next academic year?
- What happens if this is not resolved before the next enrollment or term cycle?
- When did leadership first recognize this as an issue?
- Is there a deadline driving this — budget cycle, accreditation review, or student survey results?

**Frequency & Scale**
- How often does this occur during a typical term?
- Roughly how many students or staff are affected each time?
- Is the impact increasing, staying flat, or getting worse?

**Prior Attempts & Gaps**
- What have you tried already to address this?
- What worked partially, and where did it break down?
- If nothing has stuck, what do you think prevented progress?
- Was this handled locally by departments or centrally by IT or Student Services?

**Metrics & Impact**
- Which institutional metrics are most impacted by this today?
- How are you currently measuring or tracking this?
- What feedback are you hearing from students or faculty as a result?

**Ownership & Decision Influence**
- Who is ultimately accountable for improving this outcome?
- Who feels the pain most day to day?
- Who would need to sign off on a change to how this is handled?

**Organizational Impact**
- How is this affecting staff capacity or morale?
- Where are people compensating manually or working around systems?
- What trade-offs are teams making because of this issue?

**Dependencies & Constraints**
- What other systems or processes does this depend on today?
- Where do handoffs typically break down?
- Are there governance, IT, or data-privacy constraints we should be aware of early?

**Desired Outcome**
- If this were working exactly as you wanted, what would be different for students? For staff? For leadership?
- How would you define success 6–12 months after fixing this?

**Student Experience & Engagement**
- Where do students experience the most friction today when they need support or information?
- How consistent is the student experience across faculties or campuses?
- What student feedback worries leadership the most right now?

**Staff Efficiency & Capacity**
- How much staff time is spent answering repetitive or preventable student queries?
- Which roles are most overloaded today, and why?
- If staff had back 10–20% of their time, where would it be reinvested?

**Data, Visibility & Control**
- How confident are you in the data you have about student interactions today?
- Where do you lack visibility into student needs or bottlenecks?
- How easy is it to report on this at an executive level?

**Change Readiness**
- What would prevent this from being adopted across the university?
- How do new platforms typically get rolled out here — pilot first or full deployment?
- Who would champion this internally if it delivers value?

**Financial & Strategic Alignment**
- Is this problem tied to retention, enrollment stability, or student success funding?
- Has leadership linked this issue to financial or reputational risk?
- If this solution paid for itself through efficiency or retention, how would that be evaluated?

**Decision Process**
- What criteria will matter most when evaluating a solution like this?
- Who else needs to be convinced that this is worth changing?
- What would cause this initiative to stall or be deprioritized?

### The art of discovery
Discovery is a balance. You cannot expect to ask all these questions without giving value in return. Balance great targeted questions with product information and customer success stories.

Customers want to know from us:
- How our product capabilities help them solve their pain.
- Success stories of similar customers solving similar pains with us.
- What makes us unique versus our competitors.

### Gate (Discovery)
Confirmed over email to the customer, covering at least:
- Discovered pain.
- Identified players involved.
- Why they need to buy now and a buying schedule/deadline (**complete BANT**).
- Outlined desired business outcome.
- Detailed next steps in the sales process.

➡️ Now is the right time to include **Jeroen and Menno** in the sales process (after solution fit, achieved SQL).

## Step 2: Scoping

Universities live with hundreds of pains that are NOT negatively impacting their funding, financial sustainability, or ability to operate. At the highest level, institutional pains relate to **enrollment and retention, financial health, and risk**. Lower in the institution, the language and measures change.

**Goal:** Quantify and implicate the pain found in discovery. Quantify metrics around the as-is process and define preliminary metrics around the to-be process (preliminary ROI + proof of value). This provides a cost justification.

> *"Customers don't buy on price, they buy on value."* — John McMahon

### Three written documents to create
1. **Cost justification** with as-is and to-be metrics.
2. **POV plan** that directly correlates to the preliminary cost justification.
3. **Preliminary pricing proposal** based on / related to the preliminary cost justification.

### The champion
In this step, identify the champion. The coach OR champion can help build the documents above. **Only the champion can influence the EB and set the buying criteria.** Coaches get us POVs; champions get us large deals. The three documents need to reach the EB — directly (us, in a physical meeting) or indirectly (through the champion).

**Identifying the champion:** A champion has both **authority** (seen in the org chart) and **influence** (business acumen, only experienced in a meeting). Usually the one person asking the most probing, interested questions in a group meeting. Validate by asking whether they have influence over other stakeholders in the deal.

**Why someone wants to be a champion:** To solve a problem that increases their visibility, gives them ownership of the solution, improves outcomes, and potentially leads to greater influence, recognition, or advancement. This only happens when you align to problems that directly impact the most important measures of the institution's highest authority. Study the annual reports to find which problems your champion could solve to gain status.

True champions have great business acumen. They won't attach to small pains and don't want to be seen working with unprepared salespeople. They put their reputation on the line. Be a trusted business partner. Trusted business partners prove themselves during discovery and scoping by knowing everything about the institution and doing their homework on the critical business issues.

**Trust from a champion** is a three-step loop:
1. Ask good probing discovery and scoping questions.
2. Tie the Eduface message into the desired business outcomes.
3. Confirm agreement to the messaging by asking.

Three forms of trust:
- **Business trust:** can our business deliver ROI and solve the critical business problems?
- **Performance value of the company:** will we offer good support, help adoption, and make it easy to use?
- **Experience of the salesperson:** has working with the salesperson shown they'll be an ally in making the deal real? Has it become a business partnership?

**Educate the champion:** Once you find a true champion, educate him to become an internal salesperson. He needs to know what sets us apart, what makes us the only tool that can solve the business problems, and help us set the right buying criteria so we win the POV evaluation. He also helps make the three scoping documents. You can role-play the EB meeting with the champion if you aren't attending it with him.

**Testing the champion** (with care, only after earning trust). A true champion can:
- Explain how purchasing decisions are made at the institution.
- Describe the typical evaluation process for new solutions.
- Identify who was involved in the most recent major purchases.
- Walk you through the current as-is process.
- Clearly explain why the institution-specific pain is a major pain and the implications of not addressing it.
- Explain how our solution delivers business value, how competitors deliver value, and what happened in competitor meetings.

Beyond answering, a champion actively moves the deal forward: introduces other stakeholders, influences buying criteria, shares evaluation metrics, connects us with legal/procurement, and sets up the EB meeting.

### Gate (Scoping)
The three documents (cost justification, POV plan, preliminary pricing proposal) AND a clearly educated and tested champion.

## Step 3: Economic Buyer Meeting

The EB has discretionary use of the funds — they can reallocate budget by priority and hold final decision authority. **Only the EB can confirm whether buying our product is a priority.**

The EB meeting is the **Go / No-Go stage**. Confirm the priority and the meeting goes well → you complete the rest of the process. Not a priority → you hit a wall and the process is over. Ideally meet the EB **before** the POV to frame our unique differentiators into the decision criteria. The process before the EB meeting is usually longer than after.

### Your goals of the EB meeting
1. High-level summary of discovery and scoping findings.
2. Current as-is state of the institution.
3. Negative consequences of that current state.
4. Our proposed to-be state.
5. Benefits of the future state.
6. Required capabilities to achieve that future state (the buying criteria we want).
7. Customer success stories with quantified, similar before/after metrics.
8. Description of our solution based on the criteria.
9. Confirmation of the remaining decision process steps.

(This may be a presentation done WITH the champion for the EB, not a traditional meeting.)

Be prepared: you **cannot** ask discovery or scoping questions in the EB meeting — you must already know it all. Tip: explain results they hadn't considered, both negative and positive. If possible, link the Eduface tool to the EB's personal metrics.

### Questions to ask the EB (after the presentation)
- Where in your priority list does this problem rank?
- What specific business measure does this issue most affect?
- When would you prefer to solve this issue?
- Would you be willing to allocate a budget if we can prove during the POV that …?
- Besides yourself, is anyone else required to approve a purchase this size?
- If we are successful in the POV, what are your remaining steps in the decision process?

### Questions the EB will ask us (four categories)
- **Muchness:** How much does it cost? How much to implement? How much will we gain?
- **Soonness:** How soon can it be implemented? How soon before we see ROI?
- **Sureness:** How sure are you it works in this environment? How sure are you of your cost justification? Which customer can I speak to?
- **Easyness:** How easy to implement? How easy to learn to use?

### The next step
The EB confirms he will order after a successful POV, subject only to legal review and price negotiation.

### Gate (EB meeting)
Simple: either you had the meeting and got the **Go** to start the POV with set criteria, or you didn't.

## Step 4: Validation Event / POV

Analogy: if invited to a winner-take-all competition where you wager €1,000, you'd ask which sports, what defines a win, how many competitors, how they're selected, why you were invited, etc. — to decide whether you can win or shouldn't risk the €1,000. The same goes for a pilot/POV.

**Goal:** Beforehand, make sure the decision criteria are tailored to our unique product capabilities versus competitors. Know the evaluation matrix before the POV starts. During the POV, prove the solution solves the problem and scores high in the evaluation matrix.

### Requirements before a POV can start
1. Confirmed champion helping in the deal.
2. Champion has helped frame the decision criteria and scoring matrix.
3. Cost justification built with the champion where gain far exceeds pain.
4. Verified priority, budget, authority, timing, and remaining steps with the EB.

### During the POV
1. Reconfirm the as-is and to-be process metrics from the cost justification.
2. Adjust as-is and to-be processes with new information.
3. Capture material evidence of the solution solving the customer's pain.

### After the POV
1. Summary report of findings, shared in a physical meeting with our champion.
2. The champion must agree 100% with all elements of the summary.
3. Update the cost justification if there are meaningful changes; share with the EB.

### Gate (POV)
A summary report of the POV and an updated cost justification, with material evidence of the solution solving the problem.

## Step 5: Business Case and Final Proposal

The business case is the institution's internal document justifying why they made the purchase. Write it from their perspective, as if they wrote it.

It should include:
1. Their corporate objectives.
2. Their strategic initiatives.
3. The business problem they are solving.
4. How the problem hindered their strategic initiatives.
5. Before/after use-case scenarios explaining how our solution uniquely solves their problem.
6. A final cost justification translating our unique features into the institution's value, in their terms (student scores, relieved work pressure, more feedback per teacher, etc.).
7. A well-thought-out implementation plan.

Use specific numbers wherever available. The business case becomes the baseline for measuring implementation success. Meeting or overachieving it gives the option to extend and grow contracts with more products and value.

**Buyer risk:** This stage carries the most buyer risk — the buyer is scared to make a big purchase. A good business case makes the leap easier. Mitigate further by sharing other customers who solved the same pains (reference calls) and reminding them of the final cost justification.

**The implementation plan:** Decreases buyer risk, gives structure, and creates urgency by assigning a needed start date to get the tool running before a deadline. Structure it to drive urgency:
> "Start a first assessment module on date A, inform all faculties by date B, grade the first paper by date C, achieve the required result by date D — in order to do that, finalise the contract by date E."

### Gate (Business case)
A completed business case written from the institution's perspective, validated by the champion as accurate and usable internally, aligned to the institution's highest-level priorities. Includes quantified as-is/to-be metrics, a final cost justification based on POV results, and a clear implementation plan with dates and milestones. The EB has confirmed intent to proceed after successful legal review and procurement.

## Step 6: Negotiate and Close

Procurement is typically the final hurdle. For larger purchases, procurement teams are incentivized to negotiate price down and demonstrate savings. They rarely question the need for the solution; their role is to reduce risk and optimize payment terms. If the business case, EB alignment, and cost justification are not locked before procurement engages, the deal risks being negotiated down.

**Goal:** Complete legal, procurement, and commercial negotiations without reopening scope, value, or decision criteria, and close on the agreed timeline.

**Approach:** Negotiation is not about winning on price, it is about **protecting value**. Concessions are only made in exchange for something of equal or greater value (timing, scope commitment, contract length, public reference). Never negotiate against yourself and never renegotiate the business case.

Before procurement, be able to clearly restate:
1. Why the institution is buying.
2. Why now.
3. What happens if they delay.
4. What value they expect to achieve.

If you cannot clearly articulate these, you are not ready for negotiation.

**Typical procurement focus areas:** price and discounts; payment terms and invoicing; contract length and renewal; legal and data-privacy clauses; implementation timelines and liabilities. Keep procurement focused on these and prevent scope creep or value erosion.

**Managing concessions** — all concessions must be: approved internally, remembered and documented, traded (never given). Acceptable trades:
- Discount for longer contract duration.
- Discount for faster signature.
- Discount for broader deployment scope.
- Discount for referenceability.

If procurement asks for concessions without offering anything in return, escalate back to the champion and EB.

**Role of champion and EB:** The champion unblocks procurement and keeps momentum. The EB protects deal value and resolves deadlocks when procurement pushes too far. If procurement becomes the decision-maker, the deal is already in trouble.

**Forecasting discipline:** Only deals with a confirmed business case, EB commitment, and agreed signature date belong in the forecast. Verbal reassurance from procurement does not equal commitment.

### Gate (Close)
Signed contract with agreed commercial terms, legal approval completed, and a confirmed implementation start date.
