# AI Grading for Executive Education and Corporate Training Providers

*By Eduface Team · July 2026 · 8 min read*

Executive education gets treated, in a lot of assessment tooling, as a smaller version of a standard degree programme. It isn't. The learners are different, the time pressure is different, and what they actually need from feedback is different, which means the assessment approach needs to be genuinely adapted rather than just scaled down.

## Why the learner is the starting point here

Malcolm Knowles' work on andragogy, the study of how adults learn differently from children, set out several principles that map directly onto an executive education audience: adult learners are self-directed, they bring substantial prior experience they expect to be used rather than ignored, and they're motivated by immediate, practical relevance to problems they're actually facing rather than by grades in the abstract (Knowles, 1980). A working professional taking a two-week intensive programme between board meetings isn't optimising for a transcript. They're optimising for whether what they just learned changes a decision they'll make next month.

That has direct implications for what "good" feedback looks like in this context. A working professional who submits a strategy assignment wants to know, specifically and quickly, whether their reasoning holds up and what to actually change about it, not a grade they'll rarely look at again once the programme ends.

## Where the time pressure changes the calculus

Executive education operates on genuinely different timelines than a standard academic term. A cohort might complete an entire module in a single intensive week, with an assignment due at the end and the next cohort starting before that grading would traditionally be finished under an academic-calendar turnaround. Slow feedback doesn't just risk a bad review in this context, in many programmes there's no meaningful window left to act on it before the learner has moved on to the next thing entirely. Turnaround time, covered in more general terms in our companion piece on why slow marking costs providers goodwill, is arguably even more consequential here, because the cost of a delay isn't just dissatisfaction, it's feedback arriving too late to be usable at all.

## What tends to differ in the assignments themselves

Executive education assignments skew heavily toward applied, workplace-specific work: a strategy recommendation for the learner's own organisation, a leadership case reflection tied to a real situation they're navigating, a business plan for an actual initiative rather than a hypothetical one. This raises the same grading challenge covered in our companion piece on grading business case studies for strategic judgement rather than just structure, but with an added layer: the "right answer" is even less fixed than in a standard case study, since it depends on specifics of the learner's own organisation that a generic rubric won't capture unless the assessment design accounts for that explicitly.

## What corporate training providers should actually ask for

Given how directly employer-sponsored this segment usually is, with a company paying for a cohort and expecting to see genuine capability development, not just attendance, a few things matter more here than in a standard higher education deployment. Feedback needs to be specific enough that a learner can act on it immediately, not generic enough to apply to anyone's assignment. Turnaround needs to fit inside the actual programme timeline, not an academic-calendar assumption of weeks being acceptable. And because many corporate sponsors want some form of evidence that the investment produced real capability change, not just completion, consistent, criterion-based grading that can be aggregated and reported on across a cohort has real value beyond the individual learner's feedback.

## Why the compliance picture looks different too

Worth noting explicitly: a lot of the regulatory framework covered elsewhere on this site, Ofqual's rules on marking, JCQ guidance on student AI use, assumes a regulated qualification is being awarded. Much corporate and executive training isn't a regulated qualification in that sense, which doesn't mean the underlying principles, genuine human review before a result counts, transparency about what AI is doing, stop mattering. It means the enforcement mechanism is different, contractual and reputational with the sponsoring employer rather than regulatory, and it's worth building the same rigour in regardless of whether a regulator is checking.

## FAQ

**Is executive education assessment fundamentally different from standard higher education assessment?**
The underlying grading principles are similar, but the learner's motivations, time pressure, and assignment types are different enough that a directly scaled-down academic approach usually underperforms. Feedback needs to be faster and more immediately actionable.

**Why does turnaround time matter even more here than in a standard degree programme?**
Because intensive, short-format programmes often leave no meaningful window for a learner to act on feedback that arrives on an academic-calendar timeline. Slow feedback here risks being useless, not just late.

**Does Ofqual-style regulation apply to corporate training providers?**
Only where a regulated qualification is actually being awarded. Much executive and corporate training sits outside that framework, but the same principles around genuine human oversight and transparency are still worth applying voluntarily.

**What should a corporate sponsor actually look for in an assessment approach?**
Specific, immediately actionable feedback, turnaround that fits the actual programme timeline, and consistent, criterion-based grading that can be aggregated to demonstrate genuine capability development across a cohort.
