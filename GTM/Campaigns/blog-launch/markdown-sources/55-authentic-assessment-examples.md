# Authentic Assessment Examples: 12 Tasks for Colleges and How to Grade Them Consistently

*What authentic assessment is, twelve tasks by field that career and private colleges can use this term, and the part most guides skip: how several instructors grade open-ended work to the same standard.*

By Eduface · October 2026 · 31 min read · Written for instructors, program directors and instructional designers at career colleges, private colleges and online programs

You replaced the final exam in your business program with a proposal for a real local firm. Students liked it, and the proposals were the best work the program had seen in years. Then the grades came back from four instructors. Two proposals of similar quality got a 91 and a 72, and by Friday one of those students had emailed to ask why.

> **Authentic assessment examples**
> Authentic assessment asks students to do the work their field actually does, for a realistic audience, and grades both the product and the reasoning behind it. Examples for colleges include a business proposal for a local firm, an HVAC service report, a nursing handoff note, a client email in hospitality, a code review and a financial plan for a case household. Each needs a rubric built on criteria from professional practice, and instructors who calibrate on shared samples, because open-ended products vary more than test answers.

> **Key takeaways**
> 1. An assessment is authentic when it resembles real professional work on five dimensions: the task, the physical context, the social context, the result and the criteria.
> 2. Authentic tasks tied to a student's own context are harder to outsource to AI, but none of them are AI-proof.
> 3. The twelve examples below each name the evidence, what to grade and where AI can help the grader.
> 4. Consistency across instructors comes from criteria and calibration, not from the task: anchor samples, blind scoring and written decision rules.
> 5. Not every outcome needs an authentic task. Recall and simple procedures are often better served by a quiz.

## What is authentic assessment?

Authentic assessment is assessment in which students perform the kind of work their field values, and the grader judges that performance directly. Grant Wiggins put it in one line in 1990: "Assessment is authentic when we directly examine student performance on worthy intellectual tasks."¹ A traditional test, in his words, relies on "indirect or proxy 'items'" from which we infer what the student could do.¹

The difference shows up in what students produce. A traditional test asks them to recognize, recall or plug in an answer. An authentic task asks them to produce something polished and justifiable: a report, a plan, a performance, a recommendation they have to defend. Wiggins also noted that authentic tasks are "ill-structured", with the ambiguity that real work has.¹

| | Traditional test | Authentic assessment |
|---|---|---|
| What the student does | Recognizes, recalls or applies an answer out of context | Produces a product or performance that the field would recognize |
| Structure | Well-defined questions, usually one right answer | Ill-structured problem, several defensible answers |
| What gets standardized | The items and the right answer | The criteria for judging varied products |
| What the grader judges | Whether the answer is correct | The quality of the product and the reasoning behind it |
| Typical evidence | Multiple choice, short answer, timed exam | Report, plan, memo, critique, log, recorded interaction |

*Table 1: Traditional tests standardize the answer; authentic assessment standardizes the criteria. Based on Wiggins (1990).¹*

The most useful design model comes from Judith Gulikers, Theo Bastiaens and Paul Kirschner. Their five-dimensional framework, published in 2004 and tested with students and teachers at a vocational nursing college, says an assessment becomes more authentic as it resembles professional practice on five dimensions.²,³ In the version used in their study, each dimension is a plain question.³

A strip underneath: "Authenticity is a continuum: the closer each dimension is to real work, the more authentic the assessment."]
*Figure 1: The five dimensions of authentic assessment; criteria have a special role because they connect to all the others.³*

Two points in the framework matter for colleges. First, authenticity is a matter of degree, not a yes-or-no label. Second, it is relative to the real situation: teamwork in an assessment is only authentic if the real task is done in a team.³ The framework does not ask every task to be complex either. Real problems can be simple and well-structured, and the assessment should match that.³

## What makes a task authentic?

A task is authentic when it has a realistic context, a real audience or product, room for judgment and a process the student can defend. Those four tests translate the five dimensions into questions you can ask of any assignment you already have.

[CARDS: 2]
- **Realistic context** | The situation, the resources and the time frame look like the job. A service report written from readings the student took, not from a worksheet. Open notes if professionals use references.
- **A real audience or product** | Someone outside the classroom could use the result: a client, a manager, a patient's next nurse, a hiring committee. The format is the one the field uses.
- **Judgment** | There is more than one defensible answer, and the student has to choose and justify. If every strong submission looks the same, the task is closer to a test.
- **A defensible process** | The student can show how they got there: notes, drafts, readings, a log or a short oral explanation. The process is part of the evidence, not an extra.
[/CARDS]

Most assignments can move toward authenticity with a small rewrite. The topic stays the same; the situation, the audience and the format change. The figure below shows one rewrite from a hospitality program.

The internal note in that example does a lot of work. It shows the options the student considered and rejected, which is where judgment becomes visible. It also gives the grader something to grade beyond tone, and it is the part of the task that is hardest to fake.

> **For instructional designers**
> Start from the criteria, not the scenario. Ask a practitioner how they would judge the real version of the work, write those criteria down, and only then build the scenario around them. Gulikers and colleagues describe criteria from professional practice as the starting point that should guide the other four dimensions.³

## Why does authentic assessment matter in the age of AI?

Authentic assessment matters more now because AI makes a generic essay easy to outsource, and tasks tied to a student's own context are harder to hand over. Harder is not impossible, and it helps to be honest about where the protection ends.

A prompt like "discuss the principles of service recovery" can be answered by any chatbot in seconds. A reply to a specific guest, under a specific policy, with a note on rejected options, takes more effort to outsource. A proposal built on an interview with a local owner takes more still, because the student has to supply the interview.

But a student can paste the policy, the case and their interview notes into a chatbot and ask for the email, the proposal or the note. The product can be generated; the situation cannot. That is why the most useful protection comes from evidence of process: drafts over time, a log, the student's own data, and a short conversation in which the student explains a decision.

[FIGUUR: A horizontal spectrum titled "How easy is it to outsource the task to AI?". Left end red "Easy": "Generic essay on a textbook topic". Then orange: "Case study with all the data provided". Then amber: "Task using the student's own local data or interview". Then light green: "Same task, plus graded drafts and a process log". Right end green "Hardest": "Same task, plus a short oral explanation or an observed performance". Under the spectrum, an arrow pointing right labelled "more evidence of process". Above the right two positions, a note: "Still not AI-proof: these make outsourcing visible, they do not make it impossible."]
*Figure 3: Authenticity moves a task along the spectrum; evidence of process moves it further, and none of it reaches AI-proof.*

There is a second, quieter point. AI is part of many of these jobs now. A marketing brief or a code review written with AI assistance may be the realistic version of the task. If your program allows AI for part of the work, say which part, and grade what the student adds: the choice, the check, the justification.

> **Authentic is not AI-proof**
> No task design stops a determined student from using AI. What authentic tasks do is tie the product to evidence that only the student has: their own data, their drafts, their reasoning out loud. Grade that evidence, and you grade the student. If you need certainty about authorship, add an observed or oral component.

## 12 authentic assessment examples for career and private colleges

The twelve examples below are grouped by field. Each card names the task, the evidence the student produces, what to grade and where AI can help the grader. "Where AI can help" means a draft for the instructor to review, never a final decision.

The examples are written for programs that grade a mix of written work and practical skills. Most of them produce a written artifact around a real-world task, because that is the evidence that travels between instructors, campuses and terms.

### Business and finance

[CARDS: 3]
- **1. Business proposal for a local firm** | Task: interview the owner of a small local business, identify one problem and write a 1,500-word proposal with costs. Evidence: proposal, interview notes, budget table. Grade: fit to the firm's situation, a recommendation justified by the interview, realistic costs, clarity for a non-expert owner. AI can help the grader: draft per-criterion scores and comments, and point to where each recommendation is or is not justified. The instructor checks the firm and the interview are real.
- **2. Financial plan for a case household** | Task: build a 12-month plan for a case family with income, debt and two competing goals. Evidence: client memo plus spreadsheet. Grade: calculations, prioritization of goals, risks explained, language a client can act on. AI can help the grader: comment on the memo's reasoning and clarity against the rubric. The instructor checks the spreadsheet math.
- **3. Marketing campaign brief** | Task: write a brief for a campaign for a real product, with a fixed budget and a named target segment. Evidence: brief, one persona, channel plan with budget split. Grade: audience insight, channel choices justified, measurable goals, budget realism. AI can help the grader: check that each channel choice is tied to the segment and that goals are measurable.
[/CARDS]

### Health, care and hospitality

[CARDS: 3]
- **4. Nursing handoff note** | Task: from a simulated patient case, write the end-of-shift handoff in SBAR format (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation). Evidence: the written handoff, optionally a recorded verbal handoff. Grade: critical information complete, priorities right, accurate, concise. AI can help the grader: check the written note against each criterion and flag missing elements. The instructor judges clinical safety and the verbal handoff.
- **5. Reflective practice log** | Task: keep a weekly log during a placement using a named reflective model. Evidence: log entries across the placement. Grade: analysis rather than description, links to theory, an action plan, change over time. AI can help the grader: formative comments on each entry and a flag where an entry only describes. Nobody can verify from the log that events happened as written.
- **6. Client email in hospitality** | Task: reply to a guest complaint after an overbooking, within the hotel's compensation policy, plus a note to the manager on rejected options. Evidence: email and internal note. Grade: tone and de-escalation, a resolution within policy, accuracy, the reasoning in the note. AI can help the grader: check policy compliance and tone against the rubric descriptors.
[/CARDS]

### Technical and trades

[CARDS: 3]
- **7. HVAC service report** | Task: after a diagnostic lab on a training unit, write the service report the customer would receive. Evidence: report with readings, diagnosis, recommendation and estimate. Grade: readings recorded and interpreted, a diagnosis that follows from the readings, a clear explanation for the customer. AI can help the grader: check that the diagnosis is supported by the recorded readings and the explanation is clear. The lab instructor signs off the hands-on work.
- **8. Code review** | Task: review a pull request with planted defects and write the review comments. Evidence: inline comments plus a summary. Grade: defects found, severity judged correctly, comments that are actionable and respectful. AI can help the grader: compare comments to the list of planted defects and draft a score per criterion. The instructor judges the borderline calls.
- **9. Safety incident report** | Task: write the incident report for a simulated workplace incident, such as a fall on a shop floor, with root cause and corrective actions. Evidence: report and root-cause analysis. Grade: factual and objective language, root cause separated from symptoms, specific corrective actions with owners. AI can help the grader: flag opinion in the factual section and corrective actions that are not specific.
[/CARDS]

### Law, design and education

[CARDS: 3]
- **10. Legal memo for a fictional client** | Task: for a paralegal or law program, write a memo advising a fictional client on one issue. Evidence: the memo. Grade: issue spotted, rule stated and applied to the facts, a conclusion, advice the client can follow. AI can help the grader: draft criterion-level comments that point to the passage where the rule is or is not applied.
- **11. Design critique** | Task: critique a real brand's landing page or a peer's design against a brief. Evidence: written critique with annotated screenshots. Grade: design principles used to justify each point, specific and actionable suggestions, clear priorities. AI can help the grader: comment on the written critique. It does not judge the visual design itself.
- **12. Lesson plan for a real group** | Task: for an education or trainer program, plan a session for a group the student actually teaches or observes. Evidence: lesson plan plus a one-page rationale. Grade: objectives aligned with the assessment, activities fit the learners, timing, adjustments for different learners. AI can help the grader: check alignment between objectives, activities and assessment in the written plan.
[/CARDS]

## How do you write a rubric for an authentic task?

Write a rubric for an authentic task by asking how a practitioner would judge the real version of the work, and turning that into a handful of criteria with evidence a grader can point to. Gulikers and colleagues make the same point: criteria should come from professional practice and be explicit to students in advance, because in real work people know what they will be judged on.³

Most authentic tasks share five criteria, whatever the field. The wording changes per task; the structure does not. The template below uses the business proposal as the worked example, and the weights are EXAMPLE values you would set yourself.

| Criterion (EXAMPLE weight) | Competent level looks like | Evidence the grader should quote | Common gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fit to context and audience (20) | Uses the firm's actual situation; written for the owner, not the instructor | Passages that use interview details and plain language | Generic advice that would fit any firm |
| Quality of judgment (30) | Chooses one recommendation and justifies it against at least one alternative | The sentence where the choice is made and the reason given | A list of options with no decision |
| Technical accuracy (20) | Costs, figures and methods are correct and consistent | The budget table and where the text refers to it | Numbers in the text that differ from the table |
| Defensible process (15) | Notes, drafts or data show how the recommendation was reached | Interview notes, a draft, a log entry | A polished product with no trail |
| Professional communication (15) | Format, structure and tone a real recipient would expect | Opening summary, headings, length | Essay format where a proposal is expected |

*Table 2: An authentic-task rubric template. The middle column is the descriptor; the third column is what makes a score checkable.*

The third column is the one most rubrics leave out. When a criterion names the evidence a grader should be able to quote, two instructors can compare why they scored differently instead of only what they scored. It is also what an AI grading draft needs to be checkable. Our guide on [how to write a rubric AI can mark against](/resources/blog/how-to-write-a-rubric-ai-can-mark-against) goes deeper into level descriptors and testing a rubric before a live cohort.

> **Grade the decision, not the format**
> In authentic work, students who know the format can score well on communication and still dodge the hard choice. Put the most weight on the criterion where the student has to decide and justify. Format matters, but a beautiful proposal that recommends nothing is not competent work.

If your program uses competency-based assessment with "not yet" and "competent" levels, the same template works with those levels in place of points. The [competency-based assessment](/resources/blog/competency-based-assessment) guide works one competency through two attempts with a three-level rubric, so it is not repeated here.

Highlight 1 (green) "We recommend switching the booking system before hiring, because the owner said 40% of no-shows come from double bookings" → "Quality of judgment: decision plus reason ✓". Highlight 2 (amber) a sentence quoting a monthly cost of $180 → "Technical accuracy: budget table says $210. Check !". Highlight 3 (red) a generic paragraph "Social media is important for all small businesses today" → "Fit to context: generic, no link to this firm ×". A margin note at the bottom: "Every score points to a passage. Two graders can now disagree about the same sentence." All content labelled EXAMPLE.]
*Figure 5: When each score points to a passage, disagreement between graders becomes a conversation about evidence.*

## Why is authentic work hard to grade consistently across instructors?

Authentic work is hard to grade consistently because the products vary more than test answers, and each instructor weighs the criteria a little differently. On a multiple-choice item, the answer is standardized. On a proposal, a care note or a design critique, two good submissions can look nothing alike.

Wiggins saw this in 1990 and answered it directly: authentic assessment "achieves validity and reliability by emphasizing and standardizing the appropriate criteria for scoring such (varied) products".¹ Traditional testing standardizes the item; authentic assessment has to standardize the judgment.

He also named the methods that make human judgment reliable. Large writing assessments reached adequate reliability with "model papers, training of readers, papers read 'blind' and procedures to prevent bias and drift".¹ Those four methods are still the core of calibration today.

In a college, three sources of variance show up again and again:

- **Different readings of the same descriptor.** "Professional quality" means one thing to an instructor from industry and another to one who has taught for twenty years.
- **Different weights in practice.** The rubric says judgment counts for 30 points, but one grader rewards polish and another rewards the decision.
- **Drift over a stack.** The fortieth proposal gets compared to the thirty-ninth instead of to the rubric, and standards slide without anyone noticing.

> **For program directors**
> Inconsistent grades on authentic work rarely mean a weak instructor. They mean the program asked for judgment and never agreed on what good judgment looks like. Budget the calibration session as part of the assessment, the same way you budget the grading time.

## How do you calibrate instructors on authentic assessment?

Calibrate instructors by having them score the same anchor submissions blind, compare per criterion, and write down a decision rule for each disagreement. One session of about 90 minutes before grading starts, plus a small double-scored sample during grading, covers most programs.

1. **Build an anchor set.** Pick six submissions from last term or a pilot: one strong, one weak, two borderline and two that are good in different ways. The "different but good" pair is what tests whether the rubric allows more than one right answer.
2. **Score blind and independently.** Each instructor scores every anchor per criterion, without seeing anyone else's scores. Use the rubric only, no discussion yet.
3. **Compare per criterion, not totals.** Put all scores in one grid. Totals hide the problem; two instructors can reach the same total by disagreeing on every criterion.
4. **Discuss the biggest gap first.** Find the criterion with the widest spread and ask each instructor to quote the passage behind their score. The disagreement usually turns out to be about one descriptor.
5. **Write a decision rule.** Agree on how to score that kind of case, write it in one sentence and add an example. That rule goes into the rubric notes every instructor uses.
6. **Rescore two anchors.** Use the new rules. If scores now land within one level per criterion (an EXAMPLE threshold), start grading. If not, repeat steps 4 and 5.
7. **Check for drift during grading.** Double-score a small sample, for example one in ten submissions, and compare. Wiggins described sampling as the practical answer when full double-scoring is too costly.¹

[FLOWDIAGRAM] Anchor set (6 submissions, including two different but good) → Blind scoring (each instructor, per criterion) → Compare per criterion (grid, find the widest spread) → Decision rules (one sentence plus an example) → Rescore two anchors (within one level? then grade) → Sample during grading (about 1 in 10, watch for drift) → Update rubric and anchors after the term
*Figure 7: Calibration is a loop: the decision rules written this term become the rubric notes and anchors for the next.*

| Time | Step | Output |
|---|---|---|
| Before the session | Each instructor scores six anchors blind | A filled scoring grid per instructor |
| 0 to 15 min | Collect scores, find the two criteria with the widest spread | One grid on screen, gaps highlighted |
| 15 to 50 min | Discuss the biggest gaps, quote the evidence behind each score | Agreement on what each descriptor means |
| 50 to 70 min | Write decision rules for each disagreement | Three to six one-sentence rules with examples |
| 70 to 85 min | Rescore two anchors with the rules | Scores within one level, or a second round |
| 85 to 90 min | Agree on the drift check and who double-scores | Sample size and owner |

*Table 3: A 90-minute calibration agenda for four to six instructors. The scoring happens before the session, so the time goes to the disagreements.*

> **Copy-ready decision rule log**
> Assignment: [name and term]
> Criterion: [criterion name]
> The case: [what the submissions that split us had in common, in one sentence]
> The rule: [how we score that case from now on, in one sentence]
> Example: [anchor number and the passage that shows it]
> Agreed by: [initials] on [date]
> Review: [next term's calibration session]

The log matters more than it looks. Part-time and adjunct instructors join mid-year, and on a rolling intake a new instructor may grade the same task a month after the session. The written rules are how the standard reaches people who were not in the room.

## How much grading time does authentic assessment take?

Authentic assessment takes more grading time than a test, and most of the extra time sits in reading long, varied products and writing feedback on drafts. Wiggins put the scoring cost of judgment-based tasks at about $2 per student against about 1 cent for multiple choice, and argued the gains in teaching and learning were worth it.¹ The ratio is old; the direction still holds.

The table below works through one authentic task in one term. Every number is an EXAMPLE assumption you should replace with your own. The AI-assisted column assumes instructors review an AI draft and approve every grade.

| Activity (EXAMPLE: 240 students, 4 instructors, one task with one draft) | By hand | AI-assisted, instructor approves | Assumption |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rubric and anchor set | 4 h | 4 h | Same work either way |
| Tool setup and test on anchors | none | 2 h | Assignment, rubric, feedback instructions |
| Calibration session | 6 h | 6 h | 4 instructors × 1.5 h |
| Feedback on drafts | 40 h | 16 h | 10 min each by hand; 4 min to review an AI draft |
| Final grading | 100 h | 32 h | 25 min each by hand; 8 min to review an AI draft |
| Full reads of flagged and borderline work | included | 15 h | 15% of submissions read in full at 25 min |
| Double-scoring sample for drift | 10 h | 5 h | 10% by hand; 5% plus a grader comparison view |
| **Total** | **160 h** | **80 h** | About 40 h vs 20 h per instructor |

*Table 4: EXAMPLE workload for one authentic task. Setup and calibration do not shrink with AI; reading and feedback time does.*

The 8-minute review time is deliberately higher than what a June 2026 pilot at Bath Spa University measured. In that pilot, across 435 submissions in 6 subjects graded by 13 markers, review took 2 to 3 minutes per submission.⁴ Authentic products are often longer and come with attachments, so the example assumes more. Measure your own before you plan around it.

## Where can AI help grade authentic assessments, and what does Eduface do?

AI can help grade the written evidence of authentic work: it can draft criterion-level scores and comments, point to the passages behind each score and give feedback on drafts. It cannot watch a performance, inspect a physical product or judge a live interaction with a client, and the grade stays with the instructor.

That split follows the evidence. A service report, a handoff note, a legal memo and a reflective log are written, so a model can read them against the rubric. The repair on the HVAC unit, the verbal handoff at the bedside and the conversation with a real guest are not, so the instructor or assessor has to see them.

Eduface's Paper Grader works on that written evidence. It flags uncertainty instead of smoothing it over. Every suggested grade is reviewed and approved by the instructor before it counts, and for formative feedback the institution chooses whether comments go to the student directly or only after the instructor has approved them. The AI assists. The academic decides.

Two other settings matter for calibration. In blind mode, the instructor grades first and sees the AI suggestion afterwards, which keeps their own judgment visible; in AI-visible mode the suggestion shows from the start, and an institution can make either mode mandatory. Approved grades go back to the LMS gradebook, and summative assessments get an audit trail that meets AI Act requirements.

On data, Eduface runs its own model, not a third-party API such as OpenAI. Student data is processed in the EU and never used to train AI models, and Eduface signs a Data Processing Agreement with every institution.

For programs that want an oral check on authorship, Eduface's Academic Integrity module, in beta, asks students critical questions about their own paper that they answer orally, like a short defense. It is early access, so treat it as something to pilot, not a finished part of the assessment.

> **What Eduface doesn't do**
> Eduface does not observe practical performance, judge a physical product or assess a live client interaction. It cannot tell whether an interview with a local owner took place or whether a placement log is true. It does not decide grades, and it does not replace the calibration session: the rubric, the anchors and the decision rules still come from your instructors.

## When is authentic assessment not worth it?

Authentic assessment is not worth it when the outcome is recall, a simple procedure or anything a well-built test already measures well. It costs more design time, more grading time and more effort to standardize, so spend it where judgment in a realistic situation is the point.

Gulikers and colleagues are explicit that objective tests remain useful for some purposes, and that real problems are sometimes simple and well-structured with one correct answer.³ Wiggins made the same point in 1990: the debate is not "either-or", and if the aim is only to monitor performance, conventional testing is probably adequate.¹

The cost is real for instructors too. A new authentic task needs a scenario, resources, a rubric, anchors and a calibration session before the first student submits. For a small course taught by one instructor, a well-designed exam plus one authentic task may be the better balance than three authentic tasks.

| Outcome | Better fit | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Recall of terms, codes, regulations | Quiz in the LMS | Fast, cheap, one right answer |
| Simple procedure with one correct result (a dosage calculation, a wiring diagram) | Short-answer or practical test with an answer key | Real-world task is well-structured, so a test is already authentic enough |
| Judgment in a realistic situation | Authentic written task with a rubric | Several defensible answers; the reasoning is the evidence |
| Hands-on skill | Observed practical with a checklist | The performance itself is the evidence; it has to be seen |
| Communication with a client or patient | Authentic task plus an observed or recorded part | The written part shows reasoning; the live part shows delivery |

*Table 6: Match the assessment to the outcome. Authentic tasks earn their cost where judgment is what you need to see.*

> **Before you redesign a whole program**
> Pick one outcome where students currently pass the test and still struggle on the job. Make that one task authentic, calibrate on it, and measure the grading time. One well-run authentic task teaches a program more than five rushed ones.

## What to do next

1. **Audit one assessment against the four tests.** Take your next major assignment and ask: is the context realistic, is there a real audience, does it need judgment, can the student show their process? Rewrite the weakest of the four.
2. **Pick one example from the twelve.** Adapt it to a course you teach and write the five-criterion rubric from Table 2, with the evidence column filled in.
3. **Collect an anchor set now.** Save six submissions from this term, including two that are good in different ways, with permission and identifiers removed. Next term's calibration depends on them.
4. **Run the 90-minute calibration before grading starts.** Use the agenda in Table 3 and the decision rule log. Share the log with every instructor who grades the task, including those who join later.
5. **Measure the time.** Track grading and feedback minutes per submission for one task, then decide where AI-assisted review would save time and where it would not.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are examples of authentic assessment in the classroom?**
Examples include a business proposal for a local firm, a nursing handoff note from a simulated case, an HVAC service report written for the customer, a code review of a pull request, a client email in hospitality and a lesson plan for a real group. What they share is a realistic situation, a product the field would recognize and a decision the student has to justify.

**What is the difference between authentic assessment and traditional assessment?**
Traditional assessment infers ability from indirect items with one right answer; authentic assessment examines the performance directly. In Wiggins' terms, traditional tests standardize the items, while authentic assessment standardizes the criteria for judging varied products. Most programs need both.

**Is authentic assessment the same as performance-based assessment?**
Not quite. Performance-based assessment means the student performs or produces something; authentic assessment adds that the task, context, result and criteria resemble real professional work. A performance task in an artificial setting is performance-based but only partly authentic.

**Does authentic assessment prevent students from using AI?**
No. Tasks tied to a student's own data, drafts and reasoning are harder to outsource, but a student can still paste a case into a chatbot. Grade the evidence of process, and add an oral or observed component where you need certainty about authorship.

**Can AI grade authentic assessments?**
Partly. AI can draft criterion-level scores and feedback on the written evidence, such as reports, memos, logs and plans, for an instructor to review and approve. It cannot observe a practical performance, judge a physical product or assess a live client interaction.

**How do you grade authentic assessments consistently across instructors?**
Calibrate before grading starts. Instructors score the same anchor submissions blind, compare per criterion, write a decision rule for each disagreement and double-score a small sample during grading to catch drift.

**Is authentic assessment more work for instructors?**
Yes, especially in design and grading. A new task needs a scenario, a rubric, anchors and a calibration session, and the products take longer to read than test answers. Use it where judgment in a realistic situation is the outcome, and keep tests for recall and simple procedures.
