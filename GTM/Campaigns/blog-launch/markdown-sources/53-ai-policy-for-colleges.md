# AI Policy for Colleges: A Three-Layer Template for the Institution, the Syllabus and Grading

*What a college AI policy needs to cover, in the three layers most published policies mix up: the institution policy, the syllabus statement, and how instructors may use AI to grade. With copy-ready templates for each.*

By Eduface · October 2026 · 30 min read · Written for academic deans, heads of quality, program directors and instructors at private and for-profit colleges

Your college approved an AI policy last spring. It tells students what counts as misuse and how to cite a chatbot. On the first week of the October intake, a part-time instructor emails you: she has 90 essays due back and wants to paste them into ChatGPT to draft the grades. You open the policy to answer her, and it says nothing about instructors at all.

> **AI policy for college**
> A college AI policy works in three layers. The institution policy sets governance, approved tools, data rules and who decides. The syllabus statement tells students what AI use is allowed on each assignment and how to declare it. The staff layer sets what instructors may do with AI in teaching and grading: which tools, what happens to student work, a human review of every AI-suggested grade, and what students are told. Many published policies cover the first two and say little about the third.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Split your policy into three layers with different owners: institution, course, and staff use in grading.
> - Write syllabus statements per assignment, not per course, and require one declaration format everywhere.
> - Add a staff clause on AI in grading: approved tools only, no identifiable student work in personal accounts, an instructor review of every AI-suggested grade, and a line in the syllabus.
> - At a college with rolling intakes and many part-time instructors, version the policy by intake and give every instructor the same statement set.
> - A policy describes expectations; it does not secure an assessment, and AI detection scores are not evidence.

- **The three layers**, and why mixing them leaves gaps nobody owns.
- **What real college policies say**, from three US universities and the EDUCAUSE survey.

## What should a college AI policy cover?

A college AI policy should cover three separate things: the rules the institution sets, the expectations an instructor sets for students in a course, and the rules for staff who use AI in their own work, including grading. Each layer has a different author, a different reader and a different review cycle.

Most published policies are a blend. A single page tells students not to submit AI text as their own, lists an approved chatbot, mentions data protection and suggests a syllabus line. The result reads well and leaves the hard questions open. Who approves a new tool? What may an instructor upload? Which assignment allows what?

*Figure 1: Three layers, three owners. The staff layer governs what instructors do with student work, and it is the one many policies leave out.*

The layers depend on each other. A syllabus statement that allows "approved AI tools" means nothing until layer 1 says which tools are approved. A staff clause on AI grading cannot be honored if layer 1 has no rule on where student work may go.

| Layer | Who writes it | Who reads it | What it decides |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Institution policy | Academic board or provost, with privacy, IT and legal | Everyone, auditors and accreditors | Governance, approved tools, data, integrity procedure, review cycle |
| 2. Syllabus statement | Program director sets the options; instructor picks per assignment | Students | What AI use is allowed on each assignment and how to declare it |
| 3. Staff use in teaching and grading | Dean or head of quality, with privacy | Instructors, graders, assessors | Which tools staff may use with student work, review of AI-suggested grades, disclosure to students |

*Table 1: The three layers of a college AI policy. Separate owners make separate review cycles possible.*

Splitting the layers also makes the policy easier to maintain. The institution policy might change once a year. The tool list changes whenever a contract does. Syllabus statements change every term. Keeping them in one document means every small update reopens the whole thing.

## What do published college AI policies actually say?

In the published college guidance we read, the rules for students were the easiest to find and adapt, while the clearest rules on instructors grading with AI sat in a separate document. We read three US university sources in October 2026 to see what each layer looks like in practice.

The University of Minnesota Duluth publishes draft syllabus statements for three instructor stances: embrace, limited use, and prohibit.¹ The embrace statement allows generative AI "for any assignment with appropriate credit" and makes the student responsible for checking outputs. The limited statement names the assignment types where AI is allowed and where it is not. The prohibit statement ties AI use to the university's scholastic dishonesty rules and lists banned actions, such as using AI to brainstorm or to summarize source materials.¹

The University of Nebraska at Omaha (UNO) sets faculty principles that reach into layer 3. Use of AI to grade or give feedback "should be clearly stated in the syllabus and have explicit approval from students", and instructors should offer a human alternative to students who do not consent.² The same page asks for a syllabus statement in every course with the instructor's stance, examples of permitted and prohibited use, and consequences for unapproved use.²

The University of Utah goes furthest on layer 3, with separate guidelines for responsible use of AI in grading.³ Faculty keep full responsibility for grades, and the instructor of record must review AI-assisted outputs before grades are finalized. Student work is treated as an education record under FERPA, must be de-identified before any AI system processes it, and may go only to institutionally approved tools under contractual protection.³

[CARDS: 3]
- **Minnesota Duluth: three syllabus stances** | Embrace, limited use, prohibit. Each is a fill-in statement an instructor adapts. Covers layer 2 only: what students may do.
- **Nebraska Omaha: faculty principles** | A syllabus statement in every course, plus a rule that AI grading is stated in the syllabus, approved by students, with a human alternative. Touches layers 2 and 3.
- **Utah: AI in grading guidelines** | A separate document for layer 3: approved tools only, de-identification, pilot and calibration before use, documentation, disclosure, and a full human review on request.
[/CARDS]

Survey data shows why the gaps matter. In the 2025 EDUCAUSE AI Landscape Study, the share of respondents reporting an AI-related acceptable use policy rose from 23% to 39% in a year, and only 9% said their institution's cybersecurity and privacy policies were adequate for AI-related risks.⁴ So a growing share of institutions have something on paper about AI use, and very few respondents think their data rules have caught up.

## What goes into the institution-level AI policy?

The institution-level AI policy sets who decides, which tools are approved, what may happen to student and staff data, how suspected misuse is handled, and when the policy is reviewed. It should be short enough that an instructor reads it, with the detail in linked documents that can change without a board vote.

Think of it as the frame the other two layers hang on. If the frame is vague, every instructor fills the gaps differently, and at a college with three campuses that means three policies in practice.

> **Copy-ready template 1: Institution AI policy skeleton**
> 1. **Purpose and scope.** One paragraph: why the college has this policy, and that it applies to students, instructors, staff and contractors on every campus and online.
> 2. **Definitions.** What counts as "generative AI" and "AI-assisted grading" for this policy. Keep it to five terms or fewer.
> 3. **Governance.** Who owns the policy, who approves new tools, who handles exceptions, and the name of the group that meets each term.
> 4. **Approved tools.** A link to a separately maintained list, with for each tool: who may use it, with what data, and under which agreement.
> 5. **Data rules.** What may never go into an unapproved tool (student work, grades, identifiers), and what de-identification means here.
> 6. **Student use of AI.** That each course sets its rules in a syllabus statement chosen from the approved options, and that undeclared use is handled under the academic integrity procedure.
> 7. **Staff use of AI in teaching and grading.** A pointer to the staff clause (template 6), including the rule that an instructor reviews every AI-suggested grade.
> 8. **Academic integrity procedure.** How suspected misuse is raised and decided, and the rule that an AI detection score alone is not evidence.
> 9. **Transparency.** What students are told about AI use by the college and by instructors.
> 10. **Training.** What every instructor completes before teaching under the policy, including part-time and adjunct staff.
> 11. **Review.** A fixed review date, and how changes are versioned and announced per intake.

Two headings deserve more attention than they usually get. Governance decides how fast the policy can keep up: if every new tool needs a full board vote, instructors will use unapproved tools in the meantime. The approved-tools list decides whether the data rules mean anything, so keep it in one place and date every change.

> **For heads of quality**
> Keep the policy itself stable and move the volatile parts (tool list, syllabus statement options, training material) into linked documents with their own version numbers. When an accreditor or auditor asks which rules applied to a given cohort, you can then show the exact versions in force on that start date.

## How do you write an AI syllabus statement for a college course?

An AI syllabus statement tells students, in plain language, what they may and may not do with AI on each assignment, how to declare what they used, and what happens if they do not. It works best when the college offers a small set of approved statements and instructors choose per assignment, not per course.

Per assignment matters because one course often holds tasks with different purposes. A take-home research paper and an in-class practical do not need the same rule. Minnesota Duluth's limited-use statement builds this in with blanks for the assignment types where AI is and is not allowed.¹

[FIGUUR: An annotated syllabus statement, drawn as one white card with six colored brackets on its left edge and labels on the right. 1 "Stance" (navy): one sentence on the course's position on AI. 2 "Per-assignment rule" (green): a mini table with three rows, "Weekly quiz: No AI", "Research paper: AI allowed with declaration", "Final presentation: No AI, live Q&A". 3 "Allowed uses" (green): "brainstorming, grammar checks, explaining a concept". 4 "Not allowed" (amber): "submitting AI-generated text as your own, AI-written code without a declaration". 5 "How to declare" (mint): "tool, what you used it for, what you changed, attached to the submission". 6 "If you are unsure / consequences" (grey): "ask before you submit; undeclared use goes to the integrity procedure". Footer: "One card per course, rules per assignment."]
*Figure 3: A syllabus statement has six parts; the per-assignment rule and the declaration are what students will actually use.*

The three statements below cover the stances most colleges need. They are written in American English for a US student body and are meant to be adapted, not pasted unchanged. Replace the bracketed text.

> **Copy-ready template 2: Syllabus statement, AI not allowed**
> In this course, [assignment names] must be your own work, completed without generative AI tools such as ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude or Copilot. That includes using AI to brainstorm, outline, draft, rewrite, translate, summarize sources or generate code. Spelling and grammar checks built into your word processor are allowed. Assistive technology approved through [disability services office] is always allowed. If you are unsure whether a tool counts, ask me before you submit. Undeclared AI use on these assignments is handled under the [college] academic integrity procedure.

> **Copy-ready template 3: Syllabus statement, AI allowed with declaration**
> On [assignment names], you may use generative AI tools approved by [college] for [brainstorming, outlining, feedback on your draft, grammar and style]. You may not submit AI-generated text, images or code as your own work. For every assignment where you used AI, attach the AI declaration below. You are responsible for everything you submit, including errors or invented sources that an AI tool produced. On [assignment names], AI is not allowed (see the per-assignment table above). Undeclared use is handled under the [college] academic integrity procedure.

> **Copy-ready template 4: Syllabus statement, AI encouraged with conditions**
> On [assignment names], using AI is part of the task. You will use [approved tool] to [generate options, critique a draft, test an argument] and you will be assessed on how you direct, check and improve on what it produces, not on the AI output itself. Keep a short log of your main prompts and what you kept, changed or rejected, and submit it with your work. Check every fact and source the tool gives you. Do not enter personal information about yourself or others, or confidential information from a workplace placement. [Assignment names] in this course remain AI-free.

A statement only works if students can show what they did. A shared declaration format, the same in every course and on every campus, removes the most common argument after the fact: "nobody told me how to declare it".

> **Copy-ready template 5: Student AI declaration**
> I used the following AI tool(s): [name and version, if known].
> I used it for: [for example, brainstorming topics, checking grammar, explaining a concept].
> What I kept, changed or rejected: [two or three sentences].
> I did not use AI for: [the parts of the assignment that are entirely my own].
> I have checked all facts and sources in this submission myself.
> Signed: [name] Date: [date]

## Which syllabus statement fits which assignment?

The right statement depends on whether you can see the student do the work and on what the assignment is meant to show. If the work happens unsupervised, a "no AI" statement is a request you cannot verify, so choose it for supervised tasks and design something else for the rest.

The AI Assessment Scale gives a finer vocabulary for the same choice, with five levels from No AI to AI Exploration. Our [guide to making each AI Assessment Scale level hold](/resources/blog/ai-assessment-scale-aias) explains the levels and the checks behind them. The table below maps common assignment types at a career or professional college to the three statements above.

| Assignment type | Statement to use | Closest AIAS level | Check that makes it hold |
|---|---|---|---|
| In-class practical, supervised exam, live skills demonstration | Template 2: not allowed | Level 1: No AI | Supervision; the rule is enforceable in the room |
| Take-home essay or research paper | Template 3: allowed with declaration | Level 2 or 3 | Drafts on record, declaration, a short oral on key choices |
| Business plan, case study, project report | Template 3 or 4 | Level 3 or 4 | Process log, checkpoints, presentation with questions |
| Reflective journal on a placement | Template 3, no personal data in AI tools | Level 2 | Instructor knows the placement; specific detail is hard to fake |
| Assignment on using AI in the profession | Template 4: encouraged with conditions | Level 4 or 5 | Graded on prompts, checking and judgment, not the output |

*Table 2: From assignment type to syllabus statement. Where a task is unsupervised, the declaration and the design carry the rule, not the statement.*

The tree ends in "redesign" more often than most program teams expect. That is useful information. It tells you which assignments carry risk regardless of what the syllabus says, and where an oral checkpoint or staged drafts would help. ## What should an AI policy say about instructors using AI to grade?

An AI policy should say which tools instructors may use with student work, what must happen to that work first, that a human reviews every AI-suggested grade before it is final, how the use is documented, and what students are told. This is layer 3, and it is where many college policies stay silent.

The silence has a cost. Instructors under grading pressure will use whatever is at hand. Without a rule, student essays end up in personal chatbot accounts, grades are copied from a chat window without a record, and nobody can explain a grade on appeal.

The sources we read agree on the core. Utah's guidelines say AI may support grading and feedback but "may not serve as the sole or final authority in determining grades", and that AI must not make academic misconduct determinations without independent human evaluation.³ They require a pilot comparison between human and AI-assisted grading before use, periodic spot-checks during the term, and brief documentation of the tool, the nature of the assistance and its known limits.³ UNO adds explicit student approval and a human alternative for students who decline.²

[FLOWDIAGRAM] 1 Approved tool only (on the college list, under an agreement) → 2 Prepare the work (identifiers removed if the tool requires it) → 3 AI drafts (per-criterion scores and comments against the rubric) → 4 Instructor reviews (reads the work, checks the evidence, changes what is wrong) → 5 Instructor approves (only now does the grade or comment reach the student) → 6 Record (tool, version, what AI did, final grade by the instructor) → 7 Student can ask (explanation of the grade, full human review on request)
*Figure 5: In AI-assisted grading the AI drafts in step 3; steps 4 to 7 are where the policy keeps the instructor responsible and the grade explainable.*

The table separates what a staff clause can allow from what it should rule out. It draws on the Utah and UNO sources and on the data rules your layer 1 sets.

| Staff activity | Allowed? | Condition |
|---|---|---|
| Drafting rubric descriptors, model answers, quiz questions | Yes | No student data involved |
| AI-drafted feedback on student drafts | Yes | Approved tool; instructor decides whether it goes out directly or after review |
| AI-suggested scores against a rubric | Yes, with review | Approved tool; instructor reviews and approves every grade; calibration before first use |
| Pasting student work into a personal chatbot account | No | Personal accounts have no agreement with the college |
| Final grades set by AI without instructor review | No | The grade of record is the instructor's decision |
| Misconduct decisions based on an AI detection score | No | A detection score can start a conversation; it is not evidence on its own |

*Table 3: What a staff clause allows and rules out. The line runs through data and through who signs the grade.*

> **Copy-ready template 6: Staff clause, AI in teaching and grading**
> 1. Instructors may use AI tools to support teaching, feedback and grading. Use is optional.
> 2. Only tools on the [college] approved list may be used with student work, grades or student information. Personal and consumer AI accounts may not be used for any grading task.
> 3. Before student work goes into an AI tool, the instructor follows the data rules for that tool, including removing names, ID numbers and other identifying detail where the rules require it.
> 4. AI may draft scores and comments against the rubric. It may not set a final grade. The instructor reviews every AI-suggested grade, changes it where needed, and approves it. The grade of record is the instructor's decision.
> 5. Before first use on an assignment, the instructor or course lead compares AI-suggested grades with their own on a sample of already graded work, and records the result.
> 6. AI is not used to decide academic misconduct. An AI detection score alone is not evidence.
> 7. The syllabus states how AI is used in feedback and grading, that the instructor approves every grade, and how a student can ask for an explanation or a full human review.
> 8. For summative assessments, the instructor keeps a record of the tool used, what it did, and the final grade they approved.

Point 5 is the one most often left out, and it is the cheapest protection a college has. Calibrating on a dozen papers the instructor already graded shows quickly whether a tool is close on this rubric or wildly off. Our guide on [how to use ChatGPT to grade essays](/resources/blog/how-to-use-chatgpt-to-grade-essays) includes a consistency test an instructor can run in ten minutes.

> **Why the syllabus line matters**
> A student who finds out after the fact that AI helped grade their work has a fair question about how the grade was reached. A sentence in the syllabus at the start of the term, stating what the AI does and that the instructor approves every grade, answers that question before it turns into a complaint.

## What changes for a private or for-profit college?

A private or for-profit college needs the same three layers, but it has to make them work across rolling intakes, a large share of part-time instructors, several campuses or an online division, and an accreditor that will read the policy. Those conditions change how you write, version and train, more than what you write.

[CARDS: 3]
- **Rolling intakes** | A cohort that starts in October and one that starts in January may sit under different policy versions. Give each version a number and an effective start date, and tie it to the intake, not the calendar year.
- **Part-time and adjunct instructors** | They may teach one course a term and never attend a policy briefing. Give them one shared statement set, one declaration format and one staff clause, built into the course template so the default is correct.
- **Several campuses and online** | Three campuses with three readings of "AI allowed" produce three grading standards. One approved statement set, used everywhere, makes results comparable across sites.
[/CARDS]

Accreditor and regulator review is the fourth condition. We have not found accreditor-specific requirements for college AI policies that we could open and cite, so we do not quote any here. The practical point stands without a citation: when your accreditor reviews academic integrity and assessment, it will see whatever you can show. A versioned policy, a dated tool list and a record of how AI-assisted grades were reviewed are easier to show than a set of instructor emails.

> **For program directors at multi-campus colleges**
> Put the approved syllabus statements and the student declaration into the course shell of your learning management system, so every new section starts with them already in place. A part-time instructor who adds one line to choose Template 2, 3 or 4 per assignment will follow the policy. One who is asked to write a statement from scratch usually will not.

## Who owns what in a college AI policy?

Ownership in a college AI policy should follow the layers: the academic board owns the policy, privacy and IT own the tool list and data rules, program directors own the statement options, and instructors own the choice per assignment and the review of every grade. When nobody owns a piece, it stops being updated.

| Decision | Owner | Consulted | Review cycle |
|---|---|---|---|
| The institution policy text | Academic board or provost | Deans, quality, legal | Yearly |
| Approved tools and the data rules per tool | Privacy officer and IT | Deans, procurement | When a contract or tool changes |
| The set of approved syllabus statements | Head of quality or academic dean | Program directors, student representatives | Yearly, before the first intake |
| The statement per assignment | Instructor | Program director | Each term |
| The staff clause on AI in grading | Dean or head of quality | Privacy officer, instructors | Yearly |
| Review and approval of each AI-suggested grade | Instructor of record | Course lead for calibration | Every grade |
| Integrity cases involving AI | Academic integrity officer or panel | Instructor | Per case |

*Table 4: Who owns what. Each row has one owner, so each change has someone who signs it.*

The row that most often has no owner is the tool list. It sits between IT, privacy, procurement and academics, and each assumes another is keeping it current. Name one owner and give the list a date.

## How do you roll out a college AI policy in one term?

You can roll out a three-layer AI policy in one term if you inventory first, approve tools before writing statements, and pilot the staff clause on one course before it applies everywhere. The order matters more than the speed: statements that refer to tools nobody has approved yet will be ignored.

[FIGUUR: A horizontal timeline titled "One-term rollout (EXAMPLE, 15 weeks)". Five phases as colored blocks along a week axis 1 to 15. Weeks 1 to 2 (grey) "Inventory: tools in use, existing rules, assignment types". Weeks 3 to 5 (navy) "Layer 1: policy text and governance group; tool list and data rules approved". Weeks 6 to 8 (green) "Layer 2: statement set, declaration, assignment mapping with program directors". Weeks 9 to 11 (amber) "Layer 3: staff clause; calibration pilot on one course". Weeks 12 to 15 (mint) "Train all instructors including adjuncts; load templates into the course shell; publish to students; first intake under v1.0". A diamond milestone at week 15 reads "Review date set for end of next term". All labels EXAMPLE.]
*Figure 7: Tools and data rules come before statements, and the staff clause is piloted before it applies everywhere.*

1. **Inventory before you write (weeks 1 and 2).** Ask instructors which AI tools they already use, with what data. Collect existing course rules. List the assignment types across programs. You will find the real policy is already in use, unwritten.
2. **Approve tools and data rules (weeks 3 to 5).** Agree the governance group, the institution policy text and the first approved tool list. Without this, layer 2 and layer 3 have nothing to point to.
3. **Build the statement set (weeks 6 to 8).** Adapt templates 2 to 5 with program directors. Map each program's assignment types to a statement using Table 2.
4. **Pilot the staff clause (weeks 9 to 11).** One course, one assignment, one approved tool. Run the calibration in point 5 of the staff clause and write down what you learned.
5. **Train, load and publish (weeks 12 to 15).** A short session for every instructor, including part-time staff, then the templates in the course shell and the statements in front of students before the next intake.

> **A worked example (EXAMPLE numbers)**
> A career college with three campuses, six start dates a year and 140 instructors, of whom 90 are part-time. The inventory shows instructors using five different chatbots, two with student work. The governance group approves one tool for staff use with student work and bans personal accounts for grading. Program directors map 38 assignment types: 11 get Template 2, 22 get Template 3, 5 get Template 4. The pilot on one business course shows the AI-suggested grades are within one rubric level of the instructor's on most papers but run high on two criteria, so those descriptors are rewritten before rollout. Training takes one hour per instructor, recorded for those who cannot attend.

## How does Eduface fit into the staff-use layer?

## What can an AI policy not do?

An AI policy cannot secure an assessment on its own: it sets expectations and consequences, but whether an unsupervised task was done without AI depends on how the task was designed and checked. A policy that promises more than the assessment can show will be tested first in an appeal.

[CARDS: 2]
- **What a policy does** | Tells students and staff what is allowed, per assignment. Sets one declaration format. Names approved tools and data rules. Assigns owners. Makes AI-assisted grading reviewable and explainable.
- **What a policy cannot do** | Prove who wrote a take-home paper. Make an unsupervised "no AI" rule enforceable. Turn a detection score into evidence. Replace assessment design, supervision or a conversation with the student.
[/CARDS]

Detection is the clearest case. UNO's faculty principles say that, because of the unreliability and bias of AI detection tools, using them to identify integrity violations "should be a starting point for discussion, not as conclusive evidence".² Utah's grading guidelines rule out AI making misconduct determinations without independent human evaluation.³ A policy can and should say this. It cannot turn detection into a control.

> **Detection is not a policy tool**
> Do not write a policy that relies on an AI detector to enforce "no AI". Write the rule, design the assessment so that the outcome you care about is shown under conditions you can see (in class, in a staged draft, in a short oral), and treat any detection score as a reason to talk to the student, nothing more.

There is also a limit on our side. Eduface supports layer 3 and nothing else. The hardest part of a college AI policy is agreeing on layers 1 and 2 across deans, program directors and campuses, and no software does that for you.

## What are the most common mistakes in college AI policies?

The most common mistakes are writing one rule per course instead of per assignment, leaving staff use of AI out entirely, and promising enforcement the college cannot deliver. Each has a simple fix.

| Mistake | What happens | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| One AI rule per course | A course with an exam and a project gets one rule that fits neither | Statement per assignment, chosen from the approved set |
| No staff clause | Student work ends up in personal accounts; AI grades copied without a record | Template 6, with approved tools and review of every grade |
| "No AI" on take-home work, enforced by a detector | Appeals the college cannot win; unequal treatment of students | Supervised checkpoints or an oral; detection only as a reason to talk |
| No declaration format | Every instructor asks for something different; students claim they did not know | One declaration (template 5) in every course |
| Tool list in the policy text | Every tool change needs a board vote, so the list goes stale | Separate, dated tool list with one owner |
| No version per intake | Students and staff argue about which rules applied | Version number and effective start date per intake |

*Table 5: Common mistakes and their fixes. Most come from writing for one layer and forgetting the other two.*

## Frequently asked questions

**Does every college need an AI policy?**
Yes. Students and instructors are already using AI, so the choice is between written rules and unwritten ones. In the 2025 EDUCAUSE AI Landscape Study, 39% of respondents reported an AI-related acceptable use policy at their institution, up from 23% a year earlier, so many colleges are still writing theirs.

**What should an AI syllabus statement include?**
An AI syllabus statement should include the instructor's stance, the rule for each assignment, examples of allowed and prohibited uses, how to declare AI use, and what happens with undeclared use. UNO's faculty principles ask for the stance, examples of permitted and prohibited use, and consequences in every course statement.

**Can instructors use ChatGPT to grade student work?**
It depends on your college's tool list and data rules. Utah's guidelines, for example, allow AI to support grading only with institutionally approved tools under contract, never with personal accounts, after de-identification, and with the instructor reviewing every AI-assisted output before grades are final. If your college has no such rule, write one before instructors decide for themselves.

**Do students have to be told if AI helps grade their work?**
Yes, as a matter of good policy. The University of Utah and the University of Nebraska at Omaha both say the use of AI in grading should be stated in the syllabus, and UNO also asks for student approval and a human alternative. Telling students up front answers the question of how a grade was reached before anyone has to ask it.

**Should a college AI policy ban AI detectors?**
No, but it should limit what a detection score can do. UNO calls detection results a starting point for discussion, not conclusive evidence. Write that into the integrity procedure, and make sure no student is penalized on a detection score alone.

**How often should a college update its AI policy?**
Review the policy text once a year and the tool list whenever a contract or tool changes. At a college with rolling intakes, give every update a version number and an effective start date, so it is clear which rules applied to which cohort.

**Is the AI Assessment Scale the same as an AI policy?**
No. The AI Assessment Scale is a set of levels for permitted AI use on an assessment, which you can use inside your syllabus statements. A college AI policy also covers governance, tools, data and staff use, which the scale does not address. See our [AI Assessment Scale guide](/resources/blog/ai-assessment-scale-aias) for the levels.
