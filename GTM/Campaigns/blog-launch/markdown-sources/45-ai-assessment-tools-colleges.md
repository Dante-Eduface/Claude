# AI Assessment Tools for Colleges: Grading, Feedback, Oral Exams and Integrity Compared

*"AI assessment tools" is one search term for seven different jobs, from grading a 3,000-word paper to flagging a second face on a webcam. This guide maps each category, what it can and cannot do, and how a private or for-profit college should choose, price and pilot one.*

By Eduface · October 2026 · 20 min read · Written for program directors, VPs of education and instructional design leads at private and for-profit colleges

Your VP of education wants a shortlist of AI assessment tools before the next start date. You search, and the first page mixes essay graders, quiz generators, AI detectors and proctoring software, each promising faster and fairer assessment. Your full-time faculty at one campus want help grading, the adjuncts at another want help writing feedback, and your academic integrity lead wants to know who actually wrote last term's capstone papers. Every vendor uses the same word for a different product, and buying from the wrong category can cost you a year.

> **What are AI assessment tools?**
> AI assessment tools use artificial intelligence to help create, run, grade or verify student assessments. They fall into seven categories: essay and paper grading, short-answer and exam grading, formative feedback on drafts, oral assessment, authorship checks, question generation and proctoring. Each does a different job and fails in a different way. A college should choose by the assessment it runs most, then check fit with Canvas, Moodle, Blackboard or Brightspace, consistency across campuses, data handling, the audit trail and how pricing scales per learner.

## Why does "AI assessment tools" cover so many different products?

"AI assessment tools" covers many products because "assessment" stretches from writing a quiz to deciding whether a student cheated, and vendors in every part of that range use the same label. Most buyers start with the broad term because they have not yet named the job they need done.

That breadth is a problem when you build a shortlist. A proctoring tool and a paper grader share a label and almost nothing else: they process different data, carry different risks, serve different people and fail in different ways. Put them on one feature checklist and you get a comparison nobody can act on.

This guide is written for program directors, VPs of education and instructional design leads at private and for-profit colleges: career and technical schools, private universities, online providers and professional education providers. Your constraints differ from those of a large public university. You run the same curriculum at several campuses or in many sections, you rely on adjunct and part-time instructors, you enroll on rolling start dates, and your accreditor expects evidence that a grade at one campus means the same as a grade at another.

The last column is the one that matters most. In every category, a credible tool produces a draft, a suggestion or a flag, and a person decides. When a demo skips that step, or treats it as optional, you are looking at a product that will put your instructors' names on decisions they did not make.

## Essay and paper grading: can AI grade a 3,000-word paper reliably?

AI can draft a dependable rubric-based grade for a long paper when the tool is built for academic grading and an instructor approves every grade; a general chat assistant prompted with a rubric is far less dependable. This is the category most people mean when they search for AI grading tools, and the one where the gap between products is widest.

**What it does.** A paper grader reads each submission against your rubric, suggests a score per criterion, and drafts comments tied to specific passages. Better tools show how the total was built, criterion by criterion, so an instructor can see why a paper landed at a B and not a C.

**What it cannot do.** It does not know what was discussed in class, which sources the instructor recommended in week three, or that this student has improved since the midterm. It should not settle a borderline pass or fail on its own. General assistants have a specific weakness here: in our test they responded to how well a paper was written more than to how well it reasoned, and graded a weak law essay far above the lecturer's grade.¹

**Typical tools.** Purpose-built graders such as Eduface's Paper Grader, EssayGrader.ai and CoGrader, and general assistants such as ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Copilot prompted with a rubric. In our test of eight tools on six papers with known lecturer grades, a K-12-focused grader (CoGrader) returned 10.0 out of 10 for a law essay the lecturer graded 4.4 on the Dutch 10-point scale. ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini returned between 6.8 and 7.2, and Eduface returned 5.5.¹ Two student testers and six papers make that a small, directional test, not a ranking.

**What to check.** Run three to five papers you have already graded through each tool and compare the suggested grade with yours, criterion by criterion. Upload the rubric as structured criteria rather than pasting it into a chat. Run the same paper twice and see whether the grade moves.

**Eduface Paper Grader.** Eduface reads each submission, highlights specific passages and drafts criterion-specific comments grounded in the rubric, with full rubric scoring, an explainable grade breakdown per criterion and annotations in the text. Every grade waits for the instructor's approval before a student sees it. The Paper Grader works with six subject models (Law, Economics, Social Sciences, STEM, Humanities and Health Sciences) instead of one generic model for every program.

In a June 2026 pilot at one institution, Bath Spa University (435 submissions, 6 subjects, 13 markers), Eduface's suggested grade was on average about 6 points from the lecturer's grade, and about 2 points for lecturers who had tuned the model. Eduface expresses that as an average accuracy of 94% on that measure, where accuracy means how close the suggested grade came to the lecturer's grade, not how often the two matched. Review took 2 to 3 minutes per submission.² We do not yet know whether lecturers set their grade before or after seeing the suggestion, so read it as a pilot result at one institution, not a blind test.

**What it doesn't do:** it does not release a grade without an instructor's approval, it does not come with a library of pre-built rubric templates, and it does not tell you whether the student wrote the paper. That last job belongs to a different category, covered below.

## Short-answer and exam grading: where does AI help most with exams?

Short-answer and exam grading is the most constrained job on the list, because an answer key narrows what a good answer looks like; the real test of a tool is how it handles the answers that do not fit the key.

**What it does.** An exam grader scores large numbers of short and open answers per question against an answer key. Some tools group similar answers so an instructor grades a group once instead of 300 times. Others score each answer and flag the ones they are unsure about.

**What it cannot do.** It cannot give credit for a correct but unexpected method unless the key or the instructor allows for it. It does not know that a question was ambiguous until enough students misread it, and it cannot assess a practical skill that a student only describes in words.

**Typical tools.** Gradescope (Turnitin), which our guide describes as strongest for STEM exams and problem sets, using AI to cluster responses rather than write feedback, under an institutional license.¹ Eduface's Exam Grader takes the scoring approach instead of the grouping approach.

**What to check.** Ask what happens to an answer that matches nothing in the key. Check whether the tool shows how confident it is per answer, and whether approved points return to your LMS gradebook without a CSV export. If your exams are handwritten, test with scans of your own scripts.

**Eduface Exam Grader.** Three independent AI agents grade each answer without seeing each other's results, and a fourth compares them. When they agree, confidence is high; when they disagree, the answer is flagged for the instructor. Eduface reports that this is 48% more consistent than unaided human grading and takes under 4 minutes from upload to suggested grade. Every submission has an audit trail, and the grade goes back to the LMS gradebook only after the instructor approves it.

**What it doesn't do:** it does not write the exam or the answer key; you bring both. For handwritten problem sets and heavy mathematical notation, Gradescope was built for that job, and our guide rates it the stronger choice there.¹

## Formative feedback on drafts: can AI comments replace instructor feedback?

No: AI feedback on drafts works best as a fast first round of specific, rubric-based comments that arrives while the student can still act on it, with the instructor deciding how much reaches the student unreviewed.

**What it does.** A feedback tool comments on a draft against the assignment criteria before anything is graded. Good tools follow the student across drafts, so the comment on the second draft knows what the first one said.

**What it cannot do.** It does not know the student, the conversation in office hours, or why this learner is struggling this term. It can be confidently wrong about something the criteria do not cover. And a student who receives three rounds of AI comments without any sign of an instructor learns that nobody is reading.

**Typical tools.** The general assistants your students already use on their own, which see no rubric and give the instructor no view of what the student was told. And purpose-built options such as the Paper Grader's formative feedback in Eduface, which work from the instructor's criteria inside the course.

**What to check.** Who controls release: can the instructor review comments before students see them? Can the program set the tone of feedback? Does the instructor see exactly what the student saw?

**Eduface.** Formative feedback is part of the Paper Grader, not a separate product. It gives feedback on drafts according to the instructor's instructions, across several rounds (first draft, second draft and final version), and tracks the student's progress over successive drafts. There are four feedback styles: Reflective and Socratic, Constructive and Direct, Went Well and Needs Improvement, and Supportive and Encouraging. The institution decides whether feedback goes to students directly or only after an instructor has read and approved it, and the interface labels reviewed feedback "Lecturer + AI".

> **For instructional design leads**
> AI feedback can only be as specific as the criteria it works from. A criterion that says "analysis: 25%" gives any tool, and any adjunct, little to work with. A criterion that describes what separates proficient from developing analysis in your program gives both the same standard. Rewrite the rubric before you compare tools.

## Oral assessment: what does an AI examiner add?

An AI examiner makes structured oral assessment possible at class scale: it asks the questions, adapts its follow-up questions to each answer, and hands the instructor a transcript and an evaluation to approve.

Oral exams have always suited career programs. A nursing student explaining a care decision, or a business student defending a pricing plan, shows understanding that a written answer can hide. The barrier has been instructor time: twenty minutes per student across a class of 120 is 40 hours before anyone has written a grade.

**What it does.** The tool runs a spoken exam, usually online, asks follow-up questions based on what the student just said, transcribes the conversation and evaluates it against a rubric.

**What it cannot do.** It cannot make an unstructured conversation fair. A spoken exam is only as consistent as its question set, its rubric and its alternatives for students who need them, such as students with speech impairments or severe anxiety. It also cannot judge the hands-on part of a practical skill, only the reasoning a student gives about it.

**Typical tools.** This is the youngest category on the list. Eduface's Oral Examination is in beta, and any vendor here is best treated as a pilot partner rather than a finished system. Our [guide to AI oral examinations](/resources/blog/ai-oral-examinations-higher-education-guide) covers what makes oral exams reliable and how to run one inside your LMS.

**What to check.** Can the instructor set the scenario and the rubric? Does the instructor get the full transcript, not only a score? What is the alternative format for students who cannot take a spoken exam, and how are recordings stored?

**Eduface Oral Examination (beta, early access).** The instructor configures a character and a situation: a strict thesis examiner, a skeptical investor, or a patient in distress in a simulated clinical consultation. The conversation adapts to the student's answers, with real-time transcription and assessment. It has two uses: formal oral assessment, and integrity checks on submitted work.

**What it doesn't do:** it is not yet a generally available product. Plan it as a pilot in one program, with an instructor reviewing every result, not as a campus-wide rollout.

## Academic integrity: why is an authorship check different from AI detection?

An AI detector estimates whether a piece of text looks machine-written; an authorship check tests whether the student can explain and defend the work, and only the second produces evidence about the student rather than about the text.

Both are sold under the banner of academic integrity, which is why they end up on the same shortlist. They are different categories of tool, and they should be evaluated separately.

**AI writing detection.** A detector classifies text and returns a score or a percentage. The score says nothing about what the student knows, and research has documented a specific bias. A 2023 study in *Patterns* tested GPT detectors and found that they "consistently misclassify non-native English writing samples as AI-generated, whereas native writing samples are accurately identified."³ Typical tools include Turnitin's AI writing detection and GPTZero.

**Authorship verification.** An authorship check asks the student questions about their own submission: why this argument, why this source, what would change if one assumption were different. A student who wrote the paper can usually answer. A student who did not usually cannot, and the transcript shows it. That is evidence an integrity panel can weigh and a student can respond to.

> **For colleges with many second-language writers**
> If a large share of your learners write in English as a second language, a detector's errors fall hardest on exactly those students.³ If your policy uses a detector at all, treat the score as a reason to talk to the student, never as the finding.

**What to check.** For detectors: what the vendor itself says about false positives, and whether your policy lets a score count as evidence. For verification: whether questions are built from the student's own paper, whether the instructor sees the full transcript, and how a student appeals.

**Eduface Academic Integrity (beta).** The student receives critical questions about their own paper and has to answer them orally, as in a thesis defense.

**What it doesn't do:** it does not produce an AI-probability score, and Eduface does not sell AI writing detection. If your policy requires a detector, that is a separate purchase.

## Question and quiz generation: do you need a separate tool?

Often not: some LMS course editors and general AI assistants already draft quiz questions, so check what you already license before you buy a standalone question generator.

**What it does.** A question generator drafts multiple-choice, true/false, matching, fill-in-the-blank and essay prompts from course content or learning objectives, at a difficulty level you set. For colleges with rolling starts, it can help build larger question banks so the same items are not reused for every start.

**What it cannot do.** It cannot guarantee that a question is correct, that the wrong options are wrong for the right reasons, or that the item measures the learning outcome it claims to. Generated items can contain plausible errors. It also cannot tell you which items separate strong from weak students; that takes item analysis on real results.

**Typical tools.** LMS-native assistants, such as the AI Design Assistant in Anthology's Blackboard, which suggests test questions from selected course content for the instructor to review, and general assistants prompted with course material.

**What to check.** Whether every generated item goes through instructor review before it reaches a student, whether items land in your LMS question bank in the right format, and whether you run item analysis after the first start that uses them.

**Eduface.** Eduface does not generate questions. If this is your main need, start with what your LMS already includes.

## Proctoring: what does AI proctoring actually assess?

AI proctoring assesses behavior, not learning: it watches a test session and flags moments for a person to review, such as a second face on screen, a student leaving the frame or signs of another device.

**What it does.** A proctoring tool monitors a remote test session through the webcam, microphone and screen, and produces a report with time-stamped flags for an instructor or proctor to review.

**What it cannot do.** It cannot say whether the student understands the material. A flag is a prompt for review, not a finding, and every flag costs someone time to check. It also raises questions that grading tools do not: recording students in their homes, storing video, and accommodating students whose behavior looks unusual for reasons that have nothing to do with cheating.

**Typical tools.** Remote proctoring vendors such as Honorlock, Proctorio and Respondus Monitor.

**What to check.** Who reviews flags, and how many hours that takes per exam window. How long video is kept and who can see it. How a student contests a flag. And whether a different assessment design, such as an oral exam or an in-person practical, removes the need to proctor at all.

Regulators already treat grading and proctoring as separate jobs. The EU AI Act lists AI systems "intended to be used to evaluate learning outcomes" (Annex III, point 3(b)) and AI systems "intended to be used for monitoring and detecting prohibited behaviour of students during tests" (point 3(d)) as separate high-risk uses, with obligations applying from 2 December 2027.⁴ Even if your college never deals with EU rules, it is a useful map: the two categories carry different risks and deserve separate evaluations.

**Eduface.** Eduface does not do proctoring. Its oral and authorship tools work from the other direction: instead of watching the session, they test whether the student understands the work.

## How should a private or for-profit college choose an AI assessment tool?

Choose on six criteria that weigh more heavily in a multi-campus, adjunct-heavy college than in a single university department: consistency across campuses, fit for part-time instructors, LMS fit, data handling, the audit trail, and a pricing model that matches how you enroll.

### 1. Consistency across campuses and sections

The same course taught at three campuses by eight instructors tends to produce eight slightly different standards. Your accreditor, and any student who appeals a grade, will ask whether a B at one campus means a B at another. A tool helps here only if it applies the same rubric the same way everywhere and lets someone see where graders diverge.

Eduface's grader comparison dashboard shows when an individual grader deviates from the rest of the team or from the AI suggestion, without anyone checking submissions one by one. The institution can also require a grading mode: blind mode, where the instructor grades first and sees the AI suggestion afterward to avoid anchoring, or AI-visible mode, where the suggestion is shown straight away.

### 2. Part-time and adjunct instructors

Adjuncts are often hired per course, close to the start date, and paid for teaching rather than setup. A tool that needs a day of configuration per instructor will not be used. Ask how much setup is per course and how much per instructor, and whether the program team can prepare it centrally.

In Eduface, setting up a course takes three inputs: the assignment description with its learning objectives and requirements, the rubric (Eduface generates one if there is none), and feedback instructions that set how comments are phrased.

### 3. LMS fit

If instructors have to leave the LMS, download files and re-upload grades, adoption across sections will stall. Look for LTI 1.3, automatic grade passback and no separate student login. Eduface connects to Canvas, Moodle, Blackboard and Brightspace through LTI 1.3, returns approved grades through LTI Grade Services, and students do not need a separate login. A Moodle connection typically takes one afternoon for the LMS administrator.

### 4. Data handling

Student work and grades are among the most sensitive records a college holds, and your counsel will want to know where they go. Ask where data is processed, whether it trains any model, which subprocessors see it, and whether the vendor will sign your data agreement, including the FERPA terms your college requires. Eduface processes student data in the EU, never uses it to train AI models, runs its own model rather than a third-party API such as OpenAI's, and signs a data processing agreement with every institution.

### 5. The audit trail

When a grade is appealed or an accreditor samples files, you need to show who suggested what, who changed it and who approved it. Ask to see the audit trail for one real submission during the demo. Eduface keeps an audit trail per submission, and that audit trail meets AI Act requirements for summative assessments.

### 6. A pricing model that matches how you enroll

Vendors price per learner, per submission or per instructor seat, and the same college can look cheap under one model and expensive under another. The only fair comparison is to count your own billable units first, which the next section does for one example college.

## How do pricing models compare for the same college?

Count your billable units under each model before you compare quotes, because drafts, adjuncts and rolling starts push the bill in different directions.

**Worked example (example numbers, not any vendor's prices).** A multi-campus career college has 1,200 active learners across three campuses and three starts a year. Each learner submits 8 graded written assignments a year, and each assignment has 2 drafts before the final version. The college employs 70 instructors, 45 of them adjuncts.

| Pricing model | Billable units in this example | What makes the bill grow | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Per learner per month | 1,200 learners × 12 months = 14,400 learner-months a year | Enrollment, not usage | Paying for learners who submit little; how a learner is counted at each start |
| Per submission | 9,600 for finals only; 28,800 with two drafts per assignment | Every draft and resubmission | Formative feedback triples the volume, which penalizes the use you want most |
| Per instructor seat | 70 seats | Sections and adjunct turnover | Seats for adjuncts who teach one course a year |

*Table 3: Billable units for one example college under three pricing models. All numbers are example numbers.*

Ask each vendor to price this example, adjusted to your own numbers. Two things stand out. A per-submission quote that looks cheapest on finals only can triple once drafts count, in this example from 9,600 to 28,800 units. And a per-seat quote looks cheap until you count every adjunct who needs access for a single course. Per-learner pricing is the easiest to budget, but ask how learners are counted when enrollment changes with every start.

## How do you pilot an AI assessment tool without risking a term?

Pilot on work you have already graded, with success criteria written down before the first run, in one or two programs across at least two campuses.

[FLOWDIAGRAM] Pick one or two assessments (high volume, existing rubric) → Rewrite the rubric (explicit criteria and levels) → Measure your human baseline (two instructors grade the same 20 submissions) → Back-test (run last term's graded work, compare with the known grades) → Live run in blind mode (instructors grade first, then see the suggestion) → Compare campuses and graders → Decide against the criteria set in week 1

*Figure 2: A pilot that measures the tool against your own instructors, not against a vendor's demo.*

The step that is easiest to skip is the human baseline. If two of your own instructors grade the same 20 submissions and land several points apart on average, that gap is the honest benchmark for the tool. A tool that lands as close to your instructors as they land to each other is doing its job. A vendor claim measured on someone else's assignments tells you less.

| Weeks | Step | What you measure | Example success criterion |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Choose assessments, write success criteria, sign the data agreement | Nothing yet | Criteria signed off by the program director |
| 2 | Rewrite the rubric, configure the tool, connect the LMS | Setup time per course | Each course set up in under one working day |
| 3 to 4 | Human baseline on 20 submissions; back-test on 60 graded submissions from last term | Instructor-to-instructor gap; tool-to-instructor gap | Tool gap no larger than the instructor-to-instructor gap |
| 5 to 8 | Live run in blind mode: 2 programs, 2 campuses, 6 instructors including 2 adjuncts | Review time per submission; how often instructors change the suggestion | Review time down; no grade released without approval |
| 9 | Compare graders and campuses; survey instructors and a sample of students | Spread between campuses; instructors' willingness to continue | Spread narrower than last term; most instructors want to continue |

*Table 4: Example pilot protocol for a multi-campus college. Adjust the numbers; keep the order.*

Two practical notes. Check that your data agreement covers last term's submissions before you back-test with them. And put adjuncts in the pilot group: if the tool only works for the full-time instructor who championed it, it will not work across your sections.

## Where do AI assessment tools fall short?

No category on this list can judge a hands-on skill, know the student, or settle a borderline pass or fail on its own, and in some situations a different option is simply better.

**Hands-on skills.** In technical and health programs, competence often means doing: a weld, a blood draw, a wiring job, a patient handover. None of the seven categories assesses the performance itself. In-person observation against a checklist remains the instrument. Oral assessment can test the reasoning around the task, not the task.

**Borderline grades.** A good average hides individual cases. When suggested grades sit on average about 6 points from the lecturer's grade, as in the Bath Spa University pilot,² some suggestions will fall on the other side of a pass mark. That is why instructor approval matters most at the boundary, and why borderline cases deserve a second grader, whatever tool you use.

**Handwritten STEM work.** For handwritten problem sets and mathematical notation, Gradescope was designed for the job, and our guide rates it the stronger choice.¹

**Small classes.** If one instructor grades 25 papers a term, setting up a tool, rewriting the rubric and running a pilot may cost more time than it saves. AI assessment tools pay back with volume, repeated assignments and many graders.

**Free general assistants for real grades.** A general assistant with a pasted rubric has no approval step, no gradebook connection, no audit trail and often no data agreement with your college. In our test, the general assistants graded a 4.4 essay between 6.8 and 7.2.¹ That is fine for an instructor's own experiments, and not a basis for grades on a transcript.

> **What Eduface doesn't do**
> Eduface does not generate questions, proctor exams or detect AI writing. Oral Examination and Academic Integrity are in beta. If your main need is in one of those first three categories, another vendor or your LMS is the better starting point, and we would rather you know that before a demo than after.

## What should you ask a vendor before you sign?

Ask questions that force evidence rather than adjectives: what the tool does and does not do, how accuracy was measured, where data goes, who approves what, and what the bill looks like at your enrollment.

**On the category.** Which of the seven assessment jobs does your product do, and which does it not? Which features are generally available, and which are in beta?

**On accuracy.** What is your average gap from instructor grades, on which assignment types, measured how? Was the instructor's grade set before or after seeing the AI suggestion? We ask that question of our own Bath Spa University measurement, and we do not have the answer yet.

**On consistency.** What happens when the same submission is graded twice? Can I see deviations across graders and campuses without opening every submission?

**On oversight.** Can anything reach a student or the gradebook without an instructor's approval? Can the institution require blind grading?

**On data.** Where is student data processed? Is it used to train any model, including yours? Which subprocessors see it? Will you sign our data agreement as written?

**On the LMS.** Do you connect through LTI 1.3, return grades automatically and avoid a separate student login? How long does setup take for our LMS administrator?

**On pricing.** Price our worked example. What counts as a billable unit, and do drafts count?

**On the pilot.** Will you agree on success criteria in writing before we start, including the human baseline?

For a longer list, see [our vendor evaluation questions for AI assessment tools](/resources/blog/ai-assessment-tool-evaluation-questions).

## Frequently asked questions

**Are there free AI assessment tools?**
Yes, but free tiers are designed for individuals trying a product, not for a college grading students for credit. General assistants have free plans, and Eduface lets individual instructors start free with about 20 assignments a month. Before real grades depend on a tool, check that it comes with a data agreement, instructor approval before release, gradebook passback and an audit trail.

**What is the best AI assessment tool for teachers?**
The best tool is the one built for the assessment you actually run, at the level you teach. Tools designed for K-12 can misjudge college work badly: in our eight-tool test, a K-12-focused grader gave 10 out of 10 to a law essay the lecturer graded 4.4.¹ For college instructors grading written work, look for rubric-based scoring with an explainable breakdown per criterion and instructor approval before anything is released.

**Do AI assessment tools work inside Canvas, Moodle, Blackboard and Brightspace?**
Many purpose-built tools do, through LTI 1.3, the standard that lets an external tool open inside the LMS and send grades back. Eduface connects to Canvas, Moodle, Blackboard and Brightspace via LTI 1.3, returns approved grades to the gradebook through LTI Grade Services, and students do not need a separate login. Ask every vendor whether grades return automatically or need a CSV export, because manual exports are where errors creep in across many sections.

**Do AI assessment tools train on student work?**
It depends on the vendor and on your contract, so ask in writing whether submissions, grades or feedback are used to train any model, including the vendor's own. A product's default settings are not the same as the terms you signed. Eduface never uses student work or institutional data to train AI models, runs its own model rather than a third-party API such as OpenAI's, processes student data in the EU, and signs a data processing agreement with every institution.

**Will AI assessment tools replace instructors?**
No. In every category on this list, a credible tool produces a suggestion, a flag or a draft, and a person decides. Eduface puts it in one line: the AI assists, the academic decides. What changes is where instructor time goes: less on the first pass through 200 similar answers, more on borderline grades, conversations with students and the cases the tool flags as uncertain.

## What to remember when you compare AI assessment tools

**Name the job before the tool.** "AI assessment tools" is seven categories. Decide which assessment you need help with, then build a shortlist inside that category.

**Read the last column first.** In every credible category, the AI suggests and a person decides. Ask to see exactly where that happens in the product.

**Detection is not verification.** A detector scores text. An authorship check tests the student. Only the second produces evidence you can put in front of an integrity panel.

**Consistency is a program problem.** For a multi-campus college, the value lies less in one instructor grading faster and more in the same standard across every section. Pick tools that let you see where graders diverge.

**Test on work you have already graded.** Measure your own instructors against each other first, then the tool against them. Then price your own example, drafts included.
