# Grammarly for Education Review 2026

*By Eduface Team · July 2026 · 9 min read*

We get asked often enough whether Grammarly for Education can replace a proper grading tool that it's worth answering properly, in public, rather than repeating the same conversation on every sales call. Short version: it's a genuinely strong product at what it does, and what it does is meaningfully different from what an AI paper grader does.

## What Grammarly for Education actually is

Grammarly for Education is a writing assistant built for institutional deployment, sitting on top of the same underlying technology as consumer Grammarly but with administrative controls, usage analytics, and integrations aimed at a school or university context. It flags issues across four categories, correctness, clarity, engagement, and delivery, and gives students real-time suggestions as they write, inside Google Docs, Microsoft Word, browsers, and increasingly directly inside the LMS itself.

The most notable recent development is Grammarly Authorship, now integrated directly into Canvas. Rather than trying to detect whether text was AI-generated, Authorship tracks and attributes the provenance of everything in a document, typed text, pasted content, AI-generated suggestions, edits made with Grammarly's own generative tools, and produces a report the student controls that shows how the piece of writing actually came together. That's a genuinely different approach to the authenticity question than a plagiarism-style detector, closer in spirit to the verification argument we've made elsewhere about oral follow-up questions than to flagging and scoring.

## What it's genuinely strong at

Real-time writing feedback while a student is still drafting is where Grammarly earns its reputation, and it earns it fairly. Catching grammar, punctuation, and clarity issues as they happen, rather than after submission when it's too late to act on them, has real value, particularly for students still building foundational writing fluency. The Canvas Writing Score integration extends this usefully: a student gets a quality check at the point of submission, inside their normal assignment workflow, without needing to open a separate tool. For institutions worried about whether an expensive site licence is actually being used, that kind of embedded, just-in-time visibility is a genuine improvement over a browser extension nobody remembers to open.

## What it isn't built to do

Grammarly is not a grading tool, and it doesn't claim to be one in any serious sense. It has no concept of a rubric, no mechanism for assessing whether an argument is well supported by evidence, and no way to evaluate subject-specific content quality, whether a legal argument correctly applies precedent, whether a business case recommendation is actually supported by the data in the case, or whether a lab report's methodology is sound. It improves how something is written. It has essentially nothing to say about whether what's being said is any good.

Its plagiarism detection, where available at all, is described by independent reviewers as basic compared to a dedicated plagiarism or AI detection tool, and it isn't positioned as an academic integrity platform in the way Turnitin or a dedicated detector is. That's a reasonable design choice given what the product is actually for, but it means institutions sometimes overestimate what a Grammarly for Education licence covers on the integrity side.

## Where it fits alongside a grading tool, rather than instead of one

The cleanest way to think about this is as two tools solving genuinely different, complementary problems, which is exactly the confusion our companion piece on AI paper graders versus plagiarism checkers covers from a different angle. Grammarly improves the mechanics of writing before and during submission. An AI grading tool assesses the substance of the finished piece against your own criteria after submission. Running both isn't redundant. A student whose writing has already been polished by Grammarly gives an assessor, human or AI-assisted, a cleaner submission to evaluate on substance, without surface-level errors muddying the judgement about whether the underlying argument is actually strong.

## What to actually check before assuming coverage

If your institution already has Grammarly for Education and is evaluating whether you still need a separate grading solution, the honest answer is almost always yes, unless your actual pain point is purely mechanical writing quality rather than assessment consistency, turnaround time, or criterion-based grading at scale. Conversely, if you're evaluating Grammarly specifically for its authorship transparency feature as an integrity measure, it's worth being clear that it's a transparency tool showing how a document was produced, not a pass or fail judgement, and it works best as one input into a human decision rather than an automated verdict on its own.

## FAQ

**Can Grammarly for Education replace an AI grading tool?**
No. It has no rubric functionality and doesn't assess argument quality, evidence use, or subject-specific content. It improves writing mechanics; it doesn't grade substance.

**Is Grammarly's plagiarism detection sufficient for academic integrity purposes?**
Independent reviews generally describe it as basic relative to dedicated plagiarism and AI detection platforms. Institutions relying on it as their primary integrity check are likely underequipped for that purpose.

**What is Grammarly Authorship, and how is it different from an AI detector?**
It tracks and attributes where each part of a document came from, typed, pasted, AI-suggested, rather than producing a probabilistic guess about whether text is AI-generated. It's a transparency report, not a verdict.

**Does it make sense to use Grammarly and an AI grading tool together?**
Yes, for most institutions. They address different stages and different questions, writing mechanics during drafting versus substantive quality assessment after submission, and using both is complementary rather than redundant.
