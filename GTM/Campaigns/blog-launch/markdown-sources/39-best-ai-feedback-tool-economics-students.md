# Best AI Feedback Tool for Economics Students: Feedback on Models, Evidence and Policy Arguments

*Economics essays are marked on whether students use models correctly, support claims with evidence and weigh policy trade-offs, not on how well they write. The best AI feedback tool for economics students comments on exactly that. Here is what that feedback looks like, how the main tools compare, and which one fits which job.*

By Eduface · September 2026 · 12 min read · Written for economics lecturers, module leaders and students

A second-year student submits an essay on whether the UK should raise the minimum wage. It is well written. It cites two studies, includes a supply and demand diagram and reaches a confident conclusion. It is also a 2:2, because the diagram shows a competitive labour market while the argument assumes monopsony, the studies are summarised but not compared, and the conclusion ignores the trade-off the essay itself raised on page two. The student needed to hear that on the draft. There was no time to tell them.

> **What is the best AI feedback tool for economics students?**
> The best AI feedback tool for economics students comments on economic reasoning: whether models and assumptions fit the argument, whether diagrams support the text, whether evidence is appraised rather than summarised, and whether policy conclusions weigh trade-offs. It should use the lecturer's rubric and let the lecturer review feedback before it counts. General AI assistants tend to reward fluent writing instead. Eduface's Paper Grader uses a dedicated Economics model.

## What does good feedback on an economics essay look like?

Economics is a discipline of models tested against evidence. The QAA's *Subject Benchmark Statement: Economics*, revised in March 2023, describes economic theories as evidence-based, using quantitative and qualitative data analysis or a mix of the two.¹ Feedback on an economics essay should test both halves: is the model right for the question, and does the evidence support it?

In practice, good economics feedback looks at five things:

| What the marker looks at | The question feedback should ask | Typical weakness |
|---|---|---|
| **Model and assumptions** | Is this the right model for the question, and are its assumptions stated? | Using a competitive model to argue a point that needs market power |
| **Diagram and text** | Does the diagram show what the paragraph argues? | A correct diagram that contradicts the argument next to it |
| **Evidence** | Are studies compared and appraised, or only summarised? | "Card and Krueger found X. Neumark and Wascher found Y." with no discussion of why they differ |
| **Policy evaluation** | Are trade-offs, counterfactuals and distributional effects weighed? | A conclusion that ignores the downside the essay itself raised |
| **Precision** | Are terms (elasticity, deadweight loss, incidence) used correctly? | Treating "elastic" as "large" |

*Table 1: Five things good feedback on an economics essay checks.*

Feedback matters most when it arrives in time to act on it. Hattie and Timperley's review found feedback among the strongest influences on learning when it tells students where they are going, how they are doing and what to do next.² In the 2025 National Student Survey, 83.3% of respondents were positive about the timeliness of feedback.³ The draft stage is where economics students gain the most from feedback, and where lecturers have the least time to give it.

## What is the difference between weak and useful AI feedback in economics?

Here is one paragraph from a minimum wage essay, and three kinds of feedback a tool could give it.

> **Example paragraph (illustrative)**
> "Raising the minimum wage will reduce unemployment because workers will have more money to spend. As Figure 1 shows, when the wage is set above equilibrium, the quantity of labour supplied rises. Card and Krueger (1994) found that employment did not fall after a minimum wage increase in New Jersey. Therefore the UK should raise the minimum wage."

| Feedback type | What the student is told | Useful? |
|---|---|---|
| **Language only** | "Good clear writing. Consider using a more formal transition than 'Therefore'." | No. The problems are economic. |
| **Generic** | "Good use of evidence and a relevant diagram. Try to develop your evaluation further." | Barely. "Develop your evaluation" gives the student nothing to do. |
| **Economic reasoning** | "Your Figure 1 is a competitive labour market, which predicts that a wage floor above equilibrium creates unemployment: labour supplied rises but labour demanded falls. That contradicts your first sentence. Card and Krueger's result is usually explained with monopsony. Which model fits UK low-pay sectors better, and what does the other side of the evidence say? Your conclusion needs to weigh that." | Yes. It names the contradiction, the missing model and the next step. |

*Table 2: Three kinds of feedback on the same paragraph. The example is written for this article.*

The third kind is what a good economics tutor writes. It requires the tool to understand economics, not only English.

## Why do general AI assistants struggle with economics feedback?

**They respond to how well students write.** A model trained on general text responds to linguistic polish. A model trained on academic assessment responds to disciplinary argument quality. An economics essay can be fluent and still get the economics wrong.

**They do not check internal consistency well.** The most common weakness in economics essays is a mismatch: a diagram that shows one thing and a paragraph that argues another, or evidence that contradicts the conclusion. A general tool reads each paragraph as fine on its own.

**They over-mark.** In our test, general tools returned grades well above the lecturer's, and a student who believes their draft is already a 2:1 is less likely to revise it.

**They are not set up for a module.** There is no rubric interface, no lecturer review before the student sees feedback, and every session starts from scratch.

## What should you look for in an AI feedback tool for economics?

| Criterion | What good looks like |
|---|---|
| **Discipline-aware** | Trained for assessment in economics, not general chat |
| **Rubric-grounded** | Every comment linked to a criterion in the lecturer's rubric |
| **Checks consistency** | Flags where diagram, argument, evidence and conclusion do not line up |
| **Draft to draft** | Feedback on each draft refers to what changed since the last one |
| **Lecturer in control** | The lecturer can review and edit before feedback counts; grades never released without approval |
| **Honest about limits** | Clear about what it does not assess, such as handwritten diagrams or maths |
| **Data-safe** | Processed under a Data Processing Agreement, not used to train other models |

## How does Eduface give feedback on economics work?

Eduface's Paper Grader gives formative feedback on drafts and full rubric-based grading on final submissions. For an economics module, the lecturer selects the **Economics model**, one of six discipline models, trained on the conventions of writing in economics.

**Setup.** The lecturer provides the assignment brief, the rubric (or asks Eduface to generate one from the brief) and feedback instructions, for example "always comment on whether the diagram supports the argument".

**Draft-by-draft feedback.** Students can get feedback on a first draft, a second draft and the final version. Eduface tracks how each student's work develops, so feedback on the second draft can point out what improved.

**Four feedback styles.** Reflective and Socratic (questions that lead the student to the problem, such as "which model predicts the result Card and Krueger found?"), Constructive and Direct, Went Well and Needs Improvement, and Supportive and Encouraging.

**Annotations in the text.** Comments are anchored to the passage they refer to and linked to a rubric criterion.

**The lecturer controls release.** For low-stakes drafts, feedback can go directly to students or only after the lecturer has read it. No grade is ever released without lecturer approval. Reviewed feedback carries a "Lecturer + AI" label.

## If you are an economics student

If your module does not use a feedback tool and you want to check a draft yourself, these questions do most of the work:

- **Does my diagram show what my paragraph says?** Describe the diagram in one sentence without looking at your text, then compare.
- **Have I named the model and its key assumption?** "In a competitive labour market..." or "Under monopsony..." changes the prediction.
- **Have I compared the evidence, or only listed it?** If two studies disagree, say why they might.
- **Does my conclusion weigh the trade-off I raised earlier?** If you mentioned a cost on page two, the conclusion has to deal with it.
- **If you use a general AI tool, ask for weaknesses, not a grade.** In our test, general tools gave grades well above the lecturer's.

You are not the only one using AI for this. In HEPI's 2026 survey of 1,054 full-time UK undergraduates, 94% said they use generative AI to support assessed work.⁵ Check your module's rules on what is allowed and what you need to declare.

## How accurate is AI feedback once it is calibrated?

Across UK pilots, lecturers changed an average of 5% of each final grade the AI drafted. In a pilot at one UK university covering 435 submissions, six modules and 13 markers, the AI's suggested grade came within 94% of the marker's grade on average, and within 98% for markers who had tuned the model to their standards. Accuracy here means how small the gap is between the suggestion and the marker's grade.

Those pilots were not economics-specific. Test it on your own module:

> **Pilot test for an economics module**
> Take ten essays from last year that you have already marked, including two where the diagram and the argument disagreed. Run them through the tool with your rubric. Check whether the feedback caught the inconsistency, and how close the draft mark is to yours.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can ChatGPT give good feedback on an economics essay?**
It can explain concepts and suggest counter-arguments, which is useful. As a feedback tool it tends to reward fluent writing, rarely catches inconsistencies between a diagram and the argument, and over-marks. It also processes student work on US servers by default.

**Can AI give feedback on economics diagrams?**
AI feedback tools like Eduface work on the written argument, so they can flag when the text contradicts what the diagram should show. Whether a hand-drawn diagram is drawn correctly is a check to leave with the lecturer, or with a tool built for handwritten work such as Gradescope.

**Is there an AI model trained specifically for economics?**
Eduface's Paper Grader has six discipline models, and Economics is one of them. The lecturer selects it for an economics module, and the rubric and feedback instructions set what the feedback focuses on.

**Can AI feedback tools handle problem sets and maths?**
Tools built for structured and handwritten work, such as Gradescope, are stronger for problem sets. Eduface does not read handwritten scripts or maths notation. It is built for written economics: essays, reports, policy briefs and open exam answers.

**Should AI feedback on economics drafts go straight to students?**
That is the lecturer's choice. Many lecturers release draft feedback directly and review anything that carries a mark. Eduface supports both and never releases a grade without lecturer approval.
