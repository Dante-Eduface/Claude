EYEBROW: COMPETENCY-BASED ASSESSMENT · EXAMPLES AND TOOLS

# Competency-Based Assessment: Examples, a Rubric, Tools and Where AI Fits

*How competency-based assessment works, what counts as evidence, one competency worked through with a rubric, and where AI can speed up feedback without taking the competency decision away from your evaluators.*

By Eduface · October 2026 · 15 min read · Written for program directors, heads of quality and directors of instructional design at career colleges, competency-based online programs and private training providers

It is Monday morning in your competency-based online program. Over the weekend, 180 students submitted work, because nobody waits for a term deadline here. Forty of those submissions are second or third attempts from students who got "not yet" last week and fixed what the feedback told them to fix. Twelve part-time evaluators share the queue, and every student in it is stuck on that competency until someone reaches their submission.

> **What is competency based assessment?**
> Competency-based assessment measures whether a student can do a defined task to a stated standard, not how many hours or credits they have completed. Each competency has performance criteria, usually in a rubric. Students submit evidence such as a project, a performance task or a portfolio when they are ready, an evaluator rules competent or not yet, and students who fall short get feedback and try again. AI can draft criterion-level feedback on written evidence. The competency decision stays with the evaluator.

## How is competency-based assessment different from credit-hour assessment?

Competency-based assessment holds the standard fixed and lets time vary, where credit-hour assessment holds time fixed and lets the result vary.

In a credit-hour course, everyone sits the same 15 weeks and receives a letter grade, and a C passes even if one course outcome was never really met. In a competency-based program, the unit is the competency. The student moves on when the evidence shows it is met, and not before. A C-level performance on a required criterion is "not yet".

The US federal definition shows how far this can go. Under 34 CFR 668.10, a direct assessment program is one that, "in lieu of credit or clock hours as the measure of student learning, utilizes direct assessment of student learning". Direct assessment means "a measure of a student's knowledge, skills, and abilities designed to provide evidence of the student's proficiency in the relevant subject area."¹ The accrediting agency has to evaluate the program and approve the institution's claim of how each module equates to credit or clock hours.¹ Many competency-based programs stop short of that and keep terms and credit hours, with competency-based assessment inside them.

If you run programs in the UK, you will know the same idea as competence-based assessment, and in apprenticeships the competencies are written into apprenticeship standards as knowledge, skills and behaviors, assessed at the end-point assessment. The mechanics below apply to both.

| | Credit-hour assessment | Competency-based assessment |
|---|---|---|
| What is fixed | Time: term length, due dates | The standard: competencies and criteria |
| What varies | The result (A to F) | Time to reach the standard |
| Typical outcome | Letter grade, compensatory average | Competent or not yet, sometimes with an "exceeds" level |
| Attempts | Usually one, sometimes a resit | Often several per competency |
| Main evidence | Exams, papers, participation | Performance tasks, projects, portfolios, knowledge checks |
| Who evaluates | The course instructor | Often a separate evaluator or assessment faculty |

*Table 1: The two models differ on what is fixed. Most of the operational differences follow from that one choice.*

## What does a competency-based assessment model look like?

A competency-based assessment model is a chain of five decisions: what the competency is, what criteria define it, what evidence shows it, who evaluates that evidence, and what happens after "not yet".

The most useful public map of those choices comes from the American Institutes for Research, built with the Competency-Based Education Network (C-BEN). Its CBE Program Model Map Framework describes competency-based programs as "designing curricula around specific competencies; advancing learners based on demonstration and mastery of competency; and allowing variation in the time it takes for learners to demonstrate a competency."² It groups design features into 10 categories that "generally align with the eight elements in C-BEN's CBE Quality Framework", and it deliberately makes no judgment about which choice is better.²

For assessment, the framework lists the options programs actually choose between:²

- **Who designs assessments.** The program, the competency team or individual instructors, drawing on faculty, assessment design staff, industry partners or professional certification assessments.
- **How many attempts.** Unrestricted reattempts, reattempts with restrictions such as delays or attempt limits, or none, so that retaking means restarting the competency.
- **What the summative evidence is.** Project or performance-based authentic assessments "with prewritten criteria", selected-response or multiple-choice assessments, and academic essays, papers or presentations.
- **How results are reported.** Traditional letter grades, pass or fail, or "Not yet, Mastery, or Mastery Plus (or similar)".
- **Who evaluates.** In the "disaggregated faculty model", instructional faculty support learners and "a different 'assessment' faculty assess learners' work". Some programs have employer or industry partners provide staff to serve as assessment evaluators.

[FLOWDIAGRAM] Competency statement (what the student can do) → Performance criteria (a rubric with levels) → Evidence task (project, performance task, knowledge check, portfolio) → Evaluation against each criterion (evaluator decides) → Competent: recorded, student moves on · Not yet: criterion-level feedback → Student revises and resubmits → Evaluation against each criterion
*Figure 1: A competency-based assessment model is a loop, not a line. The feedback after "not yet" is part of the assessment, not an extra.*
## What counts as evidence of competency? Examples by type

Evidence of competency is anything that lets an evaluator see the student meet the criteria: some of it is watched, most of it is written or built, and each type needs a different kind of judgment.

Six evidence types cover most competency-based programs at career colleges, online providers and private training providers. Typical examples: a medical assisting student recording vital signs on a standardized patient (performance task), a cybersecurity student's risk assessment for a sample network (project), a reflection after an early childhood placement (written reflection), a quiz on HIPAA rules (knowledge check), a dental assisting student's chairside work during an externship (observed practice), and an instructional design student's three course modules with design rationales (portfolio).

| Evidence type | How it is usually assessed | Where AI can help | Where AI cannot |
|---|---|---|---|
| Performance task | Evaluator observes against a checklist or rubric | The written part around it: a care plan, a task log, a debrief | Watch the task or judge the performance |
| Project | Rubric per criterion, often with revision | Draft criterion-level scores and comments on the written artifact; feedback on drafts | Judge whether the built thing works, unless the evidence is written down |
| Written reflection | Rubric on depth and specificity | Formative comments: does it reflect, or only describe? | Treat the account as proof the practice was safe or competent |
| Knowledge check | Answer key or mark scheme | Draft marks on short and open answers that do not match an exact key | Add much to multiple choice: the LMS quiz engine already marks it |
| Observed practice | Assessor or employer evaluator, often with sign-off | Little in the observation itself | Observe, verify identity or sign off |
| Portfolio | Overall or per-competency review, mapped to criteria | Check each piece against the criteria it claims; feedback on written rationales | Judge whether the portfolio as a whole shows competence |

*Table 2: AI can help wherever the evidence is written and the criteria are stated. Observation and sign-off stay human.*

If most of your evidence comes from workshops and job sheets, our article on [AI in vocational education and what stays with the assessor](/resources/blog/ai-in-vocational-education) covers that split in detail, including end-point assessment. This article focuses on programs where written artifacts and projects carry a large share of the evidence, as they do in most online competency-based degrees and certificates.

## Worked example: one competency, one rubric, two attempts

The clearest way to see competency-based assessment in practice is to follow one competency from rubric to sign-off. Everything in this section is an illustrative example, including the competency code, the student and the numbers.

**The competency (example).** BUS-210: *Analyze operational data to recommend a process improvement.* It sits in an online, competency-based business program. The evidence is a 1,000 to 1,500-word recommendation memo to an operations manager, based on a data set the program provides: three months of order, packing and shipping times for a small e-commerce warehouse.

**The rubric (example).** Three levels per criterion, written so that each level describes what the evaluator will see in the memo.

| Criterion | Not yet | Competent | Exceeds |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Problem definition | Describes symptoms ("orders are late") without stating the process problem or its effect | States the process problem, who it affects and one measurable effect | Also separates root cause from symptoms and says what is out of scope |
| 2. Use of data | Quotes figures without linking them to the problem, or uses one period with no comparison | Uses the data correctly to show the size and pattern of the problem, with at least one comparison | Also tests an alternative explanation against the data and states the data's limits |
| 3. Recommendation | Does not follow from the analysis, or lists options without choosing | Recommends one change, traces it to the analysis and names how success will be measured | Also estimates cost or effort and names the main risk with a mitigation |
| 4. Professional communication | Structure or errors get in the way of a manager acting on it | Clear memo structure and tone; a manager can act on it in five minutes | Also opens with a summary that stands on its own |

*Table 3: An example three-level rubric for one competency. Each cell describes evidence, not a quality adjective.*

**The decision rule (example).** Competent requires Competent or Exceeds on all four criteria. A "Not yet" on any one criterion makes the competency "Not yet", however strong the rest is. That is a non-compensatory rule, and it is the core difference from a weighted course grade: a brilliant memo with a recommendation that does not follow from the data has not shown the competency. Our guide on [how to write a rubric AI can mark against](/resources/blog/how-to-write-a-rubric-ai-can-mark-against) calls these gateway criteria and explains how to write them so humans and AI read them the same way.

**Attempt 1.** The student's memo reports that late orders rose to 14% in March and recommends hiring two more packers. It is well written. An AI-drafted evaluation, grounded in the rubric, suggests:

- **Criterion 1, Competent.** The problem and its effect on customers are stated.
- **Criterion 2, Not yet.** The passage "late orders rose to 14% in March" is highlighted, with a comment: March is not compared with January or February, and the delay is not broken down by stage (picking, packing, carrier pickup).
- **Criterion 3, Not yet.** The recommendation is not traced to the analysis: nothing in the memo shows that packing is where orders are delayed.
- **Criterion 4, Competent.**

The evaluator reads the memo, agrees with both "Not yet" suggestions, and rewrites the criterion 3 comment as a question: "Where in the process do the late orders lose their time?" They approve the evaluation. The competency is recorded as Not yet, and the student has criterion-level feedback the same day.

**Attempt 2.** The student adds a stage-by-stage breakdown. Most of the delay sits between packing and the single daily carrier pickup, not in packing itself. The memo now recommends a second carrier pickup window and names on-time shipping rate as the success measure, with a rough cost estimate. The AI draft suggests Competent on criteria 1, 2 and 4, and Exceeds on criterion 3 because of the cost estimate.

The evaluator disagrees on criterion 3. The memo names a risk (the carrier may charge for a second pickup) but offers no mitigation, so it does not meet the Exceeds descriptor. The evaluator changes criterion 3 to Competent and signs off BUS-210 as Competent.

[FLOWDIAGRAM] Attempt 1 submitted → AI drafts a level and comment per criterion, with highlighted passages → Evaluator reviews, edits one comment, approves "Not yet" → Student gets criterion-level feedback the same day → Attempt 2 submitted → AI drafts again → Evaluator changes criterion 3 from Exceeds to Competent → Evaluator signs off the competency
*Figure 2: The AI drafts each evaluation. The evaluator decides each criterion and signs off the competency.*

The rubric did the heavy lifting: each descriptor named something visible in the memo, so the attempt 1 feedback told the student exactly what to fix. And the evaluator overruled the AI on the one judgment that decided a level. That is the right division of labor.

## Why is competency-based assessment hard to run at scale?

Competency-based assessment is hard to run at scale because the work never arrives in batches, every reattempt is a full evaluation, and students cannot progress until someone evaluates their work.

**Rolling submissions.** With flexible pacing, the framework's most flexible option lets learners complete a competency "more quickly or slowly than in a traditional term".² There is no marking week. Evaluation is a permanent queue, and its length changes with enrollment, not with the calendar.

**Multiple attempts per competency.** Every "not yet" generates another submission, and each one needs the same care as the first.

**Speed matters more than in a term course.** A student waiting for a result on BUS-210 cannot start the next competency that depends on it. Where a program uses subscription pricing, in which students pay per term and take as many competencies as they can complete,² a slow queue costs the student money as well as time. Fast feedback is the condition for the model to work.

**Consistency across many evaluators.** Disaggregated faculty models, part-time evaluator pools and employer evaluators all mean that the same rubric is read by many people. If one evaluator reads "traces it to the analysis" more strictly than another, two students with the same memo get different outcomes, and the competency stops meaning one thing.

Example numbers, illustrative and not from any one program:

| Example input | Value |
|---|---|
| Active students | 600 |
| Written competency submissions per student per month | 2 |
| First submissions per month | 1,200 |
| Share returned "not yet" and resubmitted | 35%, so 420 reattempts |
| Total evaluations per month | 1,620 |
| Minutes per evaluation, including written feedback | 20 |
| Evaluator hours per month | 540 |

*Table 4: Example workload for one program. Reattempts add about a third to the queue, and the queue never closes.*

Fewer minutes per evaluation is half the point. The other half is feedback that arrives while the student can still use it.

> **For heads of quality**
> Before you change any tool, run a short calibration: give the same five submissions for one competency to every evaluator, with the rubric, and compare their levels per criterion. Where evaluators split, the descriptor is usually the problem, not the evaluator. Fix the wording, then repeat each term. Whatever tool you use afterwards, it can only be as consistent as the rubric it reads.

## Which tools support competency-based assessment?

Competency-based assessment usually runs on four kinds of tools: LMS mastery features, portfolio tools, rubric grading and AI-assisted grading. None covers the whole job.

**LMS mastery features.** The major LMS platforms each track competencies or outcomes in their own way.

- **Moodle** has competency frameworks and learning plans. An administrator sets up frameworks, builds learning plan templates and assigns them to students or cohorts.³ Students can upload evidence of prior learning, which "must be approved before the competency will be deemed to be achieved", and a course teacher can rate students against each course competency.³ ⁴ A learning plan is not marked complete automatically once all competencies are met.⁴
- **Canvas** uses Outcomes with mastery scales, which can be attached to rubrics, and a Learning Mastery Gradebook where instructors see how students perform against each outcome and can let students see their mastery scores.⁵
- **Brightspace** has a Competencies tool and a Learning Outcomes tool. Learning outcomes can be aligned to rubric criteria, and D2L states that it aims to retire the Competency tool in favor of Learning Outcomes.⁶

**Portfolio tools.** Dedicated ePortfolio platforms such as PebblePad let learners map evidence to competency frameworks and let campus or external workplace assessors review and sign off, according to PebblePad's own pages.⁷ They suit programs where one piece of evidence serves several competencies, or where employers sign off.

**Rubric grading.** LMS rubric tools put the criteria next to the submission and, in Canvas and Brightspace, connect to outcomes. They make evaluation structured, not faster: the evaluator still reads every submission and writes every comment.

**AI-assisted grading.** These tools read the written evidence and draft criterion-level scores and feedback for an evaluator to review. Quality varies most in this category, so test on your own submissions; our [guide to AI grading tools for higher education](/resources/blog/ai-grading-tools-higher-education-guide) covers how.

| Tool category | What it does well | What it leaves to you | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| LMS mastery features (Moodle, Canvas, Brightspace) | Records competency status, links outcomes to rubrics, shows progress | Reading the evidence and writing feedback | Every program, as the system of record |
| Portfolio tools (for example PebblePad) | Collects evidence across competencies, external sign-off | Evaluating each piece against criteria | Placements, apprenticeships, employer evaluators |
| Rubric grading in the LMS | Structured, criterion-level evaluation | All the reading and writing time | Small volumes, or as the front end of any approach |
| AI-assisted grading with evaluator approval | Drafts criterion-level evaluations and feedback on written work | Approving each level, observed evidence, sign-off | Written artifacts and open-answer knowledge checks at volume |

*Table 5: Most programs need the first row plus one or two others. The question is where the reading time goes.*

## Where AI falls short, and when a simple mastery tracker is enough

AI cannot observe practical performance, cannot sign off a competency, and adds little to programs whose evidence is mostly multiple choice or observation.

**It cannot see the practice.** A text model reads what a student wrote about a task, not the task. A flawless care plan can sit next to poor patient handling. Where a competency is defined by what a person does, the observation is the evidence.

**It cannot sign off.** The competent or not-yet decision is a claim your institution makes to employers, accreditors and, for direct assessment programs, the accrediting agency that approved the program.¹ That claim has to rest on a named evaluator's judgment. Keep the sign-off with the evaluator explicitly in your policy, not only in practice.

**It does not fix a vague rubric.** If evaluators disagree about what "traces the recommendation to the analysis" means, an AI reading the same words will not settle it. Calibrate the rubric first.

**Sometimes the LMS is enough.** If your evidence is mostly selected-response knowledge checks plus observed practice with checklists, the LMS quiz engine marks the first and a mastery tracker records the second. The same goes for small cohorts, where a few evaluators can keep up with the queue. AI-assisted grading earns its place when written artifacts arrive in volume, reattempts multiply the queue, and students wait for feedback they need to move on.

If you do pilot, use one competency with written evidence and 30 to 50 submissions your evaluators have already decided, and compare the AI's suggested level with the evaluator's on every criterion, not only on the final outcome. Agreement on "competent" can hide disagreement on the criterion that decided it.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is competency-based assessment the same as competency-based education?**
No. Competency-based education is the whole program design: curriculum, pacing, support and assessment. Competency-based assessment is the assessment part, and it can also run inside a term-based program that keeps credit hours. A course can assess against competencies with a not-yet-and-retry loop without becoming a direct assessment program.

**Can a multiple-choice test count as competency-based assessment?**
Yes, for the knowledge a competency rests on. The AIR and C-BEN framework lists selected-response and multiple-choice assessment as one option for summative assessment, next to projects, performance tasks and papers. Most competencies about doing something also need evidence of performance, so a knowledge check is rarely the only evidence.

**How many attempts should students get per competency?**
That is a program decision with no single right answer. The AIR framework describes three options: unrestricted attempts, attempts with restrictions such as delays or revisiting material, and no reattempts without restarting the competency. Whatever you choose, budget evaluator time for it.

**Do competency-based programs need accreditor approval in the US?**
Direct assessment programs do. Under 34 CFR 668.10, the accrediting agency must evaluate the program and approve the institution's claim of how each module equates to credit or clock hours. Competency-based courses inside a credit-hour program follow your accreditor's normal rules, so check with your accreditor before you change how competencies are reported.

**Can AI decide whether a student is competent?**
No. AI can draft a level and feedback per criterion on written evidence. The competent or not-yet decision is a judgment your institution stands behind, so it belongs to a named evaluator. In Eduface, no grade reaches the LMS gradebook until the evaluator approves it.
