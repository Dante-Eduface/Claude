# What Business Qualification Learners Actually Complain About When Marking Is Slow

*By Eduface Team · July 2026 · 8 min read*

Our companion piece on why slow marking costs private providers refunds and bad reviews looks at the business side of this problem. This one looks at something narrower and, in a way, more useful: what learners actually do when marking drags on, and why the loudest complaints you hear are probably not the ones that should worry you most.

## Complaining is only one of three responses, and it's not the dangerous one

There's an old, well known framework from the economist Albert Hirschman that fits this situation almost perfectly, even though he wrote it about entirely different things, declining firms, political parties, people deciding whether to stay in a country or leave it. Hirschman's argument was that when people are dissatisfied with an organisation, they have essentially three options: exit, meaning they leave without saying anything; voice, meaning they complain, in the hope of fixing the thing that's bothering them; or loyalty, meaning they stay and tolerate it, at least for a while (Hirschman, 1970).

Applied to a training provider, a learner who emails you furious about a six week marking delay is exercising voice. That's uncomfortable in the moment, but it's actually the version you want, because it gives you a chance to respond, explain, and fix it. The learner who says nothing, quietly finishes the course, and simply never enrols in anything else, never refers a colleague, never mentions you again, is exercising exit, and you may never even register that anything went wrong. A later refinement of Hirschman's framework, sometimes called EVLN, adds a fourth category worth knowing: neglect, where someone stays enrolled but visibly disengages, submitting less effort, showing up less, without ever formally leaving or complaining. If your only signal for dissatisfaction is complaints that land in an inbox, you're only seeing one of these four responses, and arguably the least common one.

## Why this matters more for business and management qualifications specifically

Learners on business and management qualifications tend to be adults already embedded in professional networks, often studying while working, often part of a cohort connected through an employer or a shared industry. That combination makes exit and neglect especially costly, even though they're silent. A learner who quietly disengages rarely does so in isolation. They mention it to colleagues considering the same qualification. They don't put your name forward when someone at work asks for a recommendation. None of that shows up as a complaint. It shows up, eventually, as a slower pipeline of the referrals a lot of small providers rely on more than they'd like to admit.

## What the actual content of complaints tends to reveal

When learners do exercise voice, what they say is worth listening to carefully, because the surface complaint and the real one are often different. "Marking is taking too long" is the surface version. Underneath it is usually something more specific and more urgent: a missed application deadline for a promotion that required the qualification, an employer reimbursement claim with its own paperwork deadline, or simply the accumulated anxiety of having paid a meaningful amount of money and heard nothing back. Responding to the surface complaint with a generic apology addresses almost none of that. Responding to the actual underlying stake, asking directly what the learner needs the result for and by when, does far more, and it's a fairly small operational change to build that question into how your team handles a complaint when one does arrive.

## What this means practically for a small provider

If you're only tracking complaints that reach you directly, build in a way to catch the quieter signals too. Course completion rates, re-enrolment rates, and referral rates are rough but genuinely useful proxies for the exit and neglect responses you'd otherwise never see. A drop in any of them, even a small one, following a period of slow marking is worth investigating specifically, rather than being absorbed into a general sense that "enrolment's a bit soft this quarter."

When a complaint does arrive, treat it as valuable information rather than just a problem to close out quickly. Hirschman's own point about loyalty is relevant here too: learners who voice a complaint are often, in a real sense, the ones still invested enough in the relationship to bother. That's worth recognising in how you respond, not just resolving.

## FAQ

**Are complaints the biggest risk from slow marking?**
Not necessarily. The learners who complain are telling you something you can act on. The ones who quietly leave, or quietly disengage without ever complaining, are often the bigger long-term risk, precisely because you don't see them.

**What's a practical way to catch the "silent" version of dissatisfaction?**
Track completion rates, re-enrolment rates, and referral rates, and treat a dip in any of them following a slow marking period as a signal worth investigating, not just background noise.

**What should we actually ask when a learner complains about turnaround time?**
Ask what they need the result for and by when. The stated complaint is often about the delay itself, but the real urgency is usually a specific downstream deadline the learner is tracking closely.

**Does this framework apply differently to business qualifications than other subjects?**
The underlying pattern is general, but business and management learners are often embedded in professional networks where quiet dissatisfaction spreads through word of mouth particularly effectively, which raises the stakes of missing the silent responses.
