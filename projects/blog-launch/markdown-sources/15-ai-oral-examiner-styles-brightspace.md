# AI Oral Examiner Styles in Brightspace: Socratic, Confrontational, Supportive and Formal Explained

*By Eduface Team · July 2026 · 8 min read*

As oral exams make a comeback as a harder-to-fake alternative to written AI-generated work, a question that gets far less attention than it deserves is what kind of examiner should actually be asking the questions. It's tempting to treat the choice of examiner tone as a cosmetic detail, something you pick once and forget about. It isn't. The dialogue style an oral exam uses genuinely changes what a student is able to demonstrate, and getting it wrong doesn't just make the exam feel unpleasant, it can quietly distort what you're actually measuring.

## Why tone isn't just a style choice

There's a well known finding from organisational psychology that, on the surface, has nothing to do with exams. Amy Edmondson's 1999 study of work teams introduced the concept of psychological safety, the shared belief that it's safe to take an interpersonal risk, to admit uncertainty, to think out loud, without being punished for it, and found that teams with higher psychological safety showed measurably more learning behaviour, more willingness to surface problems, ask questions, and reason through difficulty in front of others (Edmondson, 1999). The research was about manufacturing teams, not exams, but the underlying mechanism transfers directly to an oral assessment. A student who feels safe reasoning aloud, including thinking through a wrong turn before correcting it, will show you more of their actual understanding than a student who's guarded because they expect every hesitation to be punished.

That doesn't mean every oral exam should be gentle. It means the dialogue style needs to match what you're actually trying to assess, deliberately, rather than defaulting to whichever tone happens to be the examiner's personal habit.

## The four dialogue styles, and what each one is actually good for

Eduface's Oral Examination tool lets a lecturer set the AI examiner's character before the session starts, choosing from four distinct dialogue styles.

**Socratic** asks open-ended, probing questions that build toward deeper reasoning, without pushing back aggressively on a wrong answer. This is the closest match to Edmondson's high-safety condition, and it tends to be the right default for formative vivas or early-stage assessment, where the goal is to see how a student thinks, not just whether they arrive at the right answer.

**Confrontational** pushes back hard on every claim, forcing the student to defend their position under real pressure. This is deliberately closer to a low-safety condition, and that's the point: for assessments specifically meant to test resilience under pressure, a mock client negotiation, a dissertation defence where the whole exercise is defending a position against genuine challenge, removing the pressure would undermine what's actually being tested.

**Supportive** encourages and guides, drawing the best answer out of a student rather than testing whether they can perform under duress. This suits lower-stakes or early-programme assessment where the goal is building confidence with the oral format itself, which connects directly to the practice and rehearsal use case covered in our companion piece on Moodle.

**Formal** maintains a strict academic tone and procedural structure throughout, closest to a traditional, neutral viva. This is often the safest default for a high-stakes, summative assessment where you want the tone itself to be a non-factor in the result.

## Why the choice needs to be deliberate, not habitual

The risk with a human examiner is that their natural style, warm, brusque, painstakingly formal, tends to stay fixed regardless of what the specific assessment is meant to test, simply because it's their personality rather than a deliberate choice. That's exactly the inconsistency our companion piece on Canvas oral exams discusses in the context of grading criteria, and the same logic applies to tone. An examiner who is naturally confrontational will produce a harder experience across every viva they run, whether or not resilience under pressure was ever meant to be part of what's being tested.

Choosing the AI examiner's style deliberately, matched to what the specific assessment is actually meant to measure, removes that accidental variability. It doesn't mean every exam should be Socratic and gentle. It means the choice should be made on purpose, assessment by assessment, rather than inherited from whoever happens to be running the session that day.

## FAQ

**What are the four AI examiner dialogue styles available for oral exams?**
Socratic, Confrontational, Supportive, and Formal. Each is suited to a different assessment purpose, from low-pressure rehearsal through to a high-stakes summative viva, and the right choice depends on what the specific assessment is meant to measure.

**Does a harsher dialogue style produce a harsher grade?**
Not directly. The dialogue style affects how questions are asked and how much pressure a student experiences during the conversation. The grade itself is applied against the lecturer's own criteria, reviewed and approved before release, regardless of which style was used to conduct the exam.

**Which dialogue style should we use for a standard summative viva?**
Formal is usually the safest default for high-stakes, standard assessment, since it keeps tone itself out of the equation. Confrontational is better reserved for assessments specifically designed to test performance under pressure.

**Can we change the dialogue style between different assessments?**
Yes. It's set per session, so a lecturer can use a Supportive or Socratic style for formative or practice contexts and switch to Formal or Confrontational for a specific summative assessment designed to test resilience.

**Do we need separate setup for each dialogue style in Brightspace?**
No. All four styles are available within the same LTI 1.3 integration. A lecturer selects the style as part of configuring each session.
